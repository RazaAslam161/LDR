# data (revised)

## What changed vs v1
FATAL 1 — "the drift invariant covers DDL only; buckets, cron jobs and retention rows are DATA and are undetectable." CLOSED, by splitting one check into two that share nothing. STRUCTURE (DDL) stays with `supabase db diff`. CONFORMANCE (rows, flags, grants, settings) becomes `conformance_check()`, a read-only SECURITY DEFINER function that re-derives ~14 classes of fact from the live catalogue (`storage.buckets.public`, `cron.job.active`+schedule, `retention_policy` contents, `pg_class.relrowsecurity`, `pg_policies` shape, table/column privileges, `pg_publication_tables`, `pg_indexes`, `pg_trigger`, partition runway, `current_setting('app.*')`, extension set, exemption expiry, ledger checksums) and compares each against a checked-in `ops_expectation` row. It runs INSIDE production every 60 s from `ops_tick`, so a 2am bucket flip is an open `ops_alert` within one minute — and it needs no CI credential, which also closes flaw 13(a) (the nightly prod diff no longer justifies giving a GitHub runner the owner password; it runs as a catalogue-only read-only role). The sentence "a retention rule is reviewed, diffed and rolled back like any other change" is withdrawn and replaced: a retention rule is reviewed, *asserted* and rolled back. The one thing an internal check cannot see is its own unscheduling, which is why the external dead-man's-switch pinger (shared with push.md §8) is mandatory and alarms on silence, not on a message.

FATAL 2 — "a 10 s pg_cron outbox drain adds up to 10 s to a 30 s call ring." CLOSED by deleting my version and adopting revised/push.md verbatim. The outbox is the RETRY owner, not the primary path: a statement-level AFTER INSERT trigger fires an immediate empty-bodied `net.http_post` wake (advisory-lock debounced) for `priority_rank <= 1` (`call`, `reach`); the 10 s cron is the floor for `care`/`message` only and is a declared, SLI'd tradeoff. `call` TTL is 45 s, not 30 s or 0 s. This domain no longer specifies push egress at all; it owns three things about it — the cron budget (`push_drain` is one of two jobs in the whole project), the daily-partition maintenance the outbox depends on, and the CI assertion that the wake trigger exists for every class with `priority_rank <= 1`.

FATAL 3 — "`state='sent'` on an HTTP response is the same lie as reach-notify." CLOSED, and generalised past push so it cannot recur elsewhere. The rule is now an invariant of the domain: an obligation row is written by the party that *caused* the work, in the same transaction; an evidence row is written by the party that *did* the work; a proxy's HTTP status is neither and may never drive a terminal state. Concretely I adopt push.md's states (`pending|inflight|sent|superseded|dead`) but the health SLI is not `count(state='dead')` — it is `accepted_not_delivered = outbox rows with fcm_accepted_at older than N minutes and no matching push_receipts row`, segmented by `Build.MANUFACTURER`, and `UNREGISTERED` is terminal-for-that-device (delete the `push_devices` row) and never dead-letters, so the one alarm the design defines does not go permanently red on reinstalls and get muted.

FATAL 4 — "`presence(couple_id, updated_at desc)` destroys HOT on the most-written table." CLOSED by deletion and by a general rule. The index is gone; `presence` is not indexed, it is RETIRED (presence.md stages K/L/M) and replaced by `presence_session(user_id pk, couple_id, last_confirmed_at)` where no indexed column is ever updated. The generalisation is the part that matters: every table declares a write profile in `table_write_profile(table_name, hot_columns[])`, CI fails if any index on that table includes a hot column, and `n_tup_hot_upd / n_tup_upd` from `pg_stat_user_tables` becomes a standing SLI with a 0.9 floor — with a NEGATIVE test that adds the bad index and asserts the ratio collapses, because an alarm never fired in the failing direction is not an alarm.

SERIOUS 4 (baseline cannot be replayed; `migration repair` runs before anything proves it) — CLOSED by reordering and narrowing. Baseline is `--schema public` ONLY (no `auth`, no `storage` — they collide with the local stack's own gotrue/storage migrations and on `ALTER ... OWNER TO supabase_admin`, and putting `storage` in diff scope makes every Supabase-side storage-api upgrade a false nightly alarm). Order is dump → replay clean on staging, amending freely because it is applied nowhere → only then `migration repair` production. `storage.buckets` rows and `storage.objects` policies become conformance expectations, not diff surface. A `schema_migration_checksum` side table closes the gap that `supabase_migrations.schema_migrations` carries no checksum.

SERIOUS 5 (all four pg_policies assertions are vacuous for a table with zero policies) — CLOSED, and the sequencing changed. The first assertion is over `pg_class.relrowsecurity` AND policy count ≥ 1, with a committed exemption list carrying `reason` and `expires_at`. `data_subject_map` completeness is sourced from ALL tables in `public`, not RLS-enabled ones. And a new Stage D0 runs a read-only production CENSUS *before* any baseline is taken, so `cycle_events`, `cycle_settings`, `love_reasons`, `care_nudges` and `app_secrets` are inspected rather than blessed; anything the census finds open (RLS off, `app_secrets` readable by `authenticated`) is closed by hand in D0a and is therefore *inside* the baseline rather than a migration racing it.

SERIOUS 6 (retention keys off client-writable `created_at`; a future-dated row is immortal) — CLOSED by a privilege manifest rather than a per-column patch. Stage D4 declares explicit table-level GRANT/REVOKE for every table in `public` — the repo currently contains none, so Supabase's default full INSERT for `authenticated` stands — and revokes INSERT/UPDATE on every server-stamped timestamp, which then gets a BEFORE trigger. A registry entry cannot be enabled unless its `ts_column` is (a) trigger-stamped and (b) column-revoked from `authenticated`, asserted by joining `retention_policy` to `pg_trigger` and `information_schema.column_privileges`. Where a monotone server integer exists (`cseq`), retention keys off that instead of a timestamp.

SERIOUS 7 (the sweep is bounded in rows, not work; no index required) — CLOSED. A registry entry requires a btree index whose LEADING column is the `ts_column`, added in the same migration that inserts the row; the sweeper refuses an entry without one and writes a `page`-severity `ops_event` instead of silently sequential-scanning. Entries whose table is daily-partitioned use `mode='partition_drop'` and never DELETE at all.

SERIOUS 8 ("private bucket closes the root cause" is obscurity; `realtime.messages` RLS is absent and belongs here) — CLOSED by demoting the claim and adding the missing thing. `realtime.messages` policies are Stage D5 and land in ONE migration shared with transport Stage 1a and presence Stage C (three siblings each planned their own; three separate policy sets on one table is a merge conflict at best and a hole at worst). The bucket change is restated as defence in depth. The 30-day public fallback is deleted: stored public URLs are normalised to object paths by a data migration, the client learns to resolve paths, then the flag flips once with nothing left readable. The durable fix is crypto.md's encrypted bytes under 32-random-byte paths, which makes a leaked URL worthless; that is named as the root fix and this domain's change is named as the interim.

SERIOUS 9 (no cancel path; couple-scoped purge semantics undecided while the purge ships) — CLOSED. `cancelled` is a state; `cancel_my_deletion()` and `account_state()` are SECURITY DEFINER RPCs that deliberately do NOT route through `current_user_couple_id()`, so they are reachable from the revoked shell, which renders "scheduled for deletion on <date> — cancel". Per-table `scope` is decided and committed in D8 BEFORE the purge job is enabled; couple-scoped rows survive until every member is purged. The partner is notified at `requested` via a durable `couple_notice` row returned by their next bootstrap — not via push, so it does not depend on delivery.

SERIOUS 10 (cost omits egress; volume model off ~7x; partitioning trigger contradicts messaging.md) — CLOSED by adopting messaging.md's model and withdrawing mine. 40 user-messages/user/day, not 7. `messages` is HASH-partitioned on `couple_id` into 32 partitions during messaging.md Stage 1, at any scale, so "partition at 100,000 users" was wrong twice: it happens at stage 1, and HASH-on-couple_id is not a retention mechanism, so DROP PARTITION never applies to `messages` (retention on chat stays deliberately zero). The attack's 200–400 GB/month presence-poll egress is real against TODAY and against my v1 — which indexed *for* that poll — and is removed by presence.md Stage F deleting the poll entirely; I do not re-price it, I state that the v1 index existed to serve a query that another domain deletes. The private-bucket "delta" of +$1.20/+$12/+$105 is withdrawn as double-counting: the revised media topology resolves objects per user under RLS regardless, so uncached is the baseline and messaging.md already prices it.

SERIOUS 11 (the static column GRANT on `profiles` is frozen by pg_dump and the next ADD COLUMN dead-ends) — CLOSED by removing the surface, not by guarding it. `guard_couple_id` (a SECURITY INVOKER trigger) already refuses the couple_id transition, so the column ACL on `profiles` is dropped and table-level UPDATE re-granted. Where a column ACL genuinely has no trigger equivalent (`capsules.unlocked_at`, `vault_pin.pin_hash`) it stays, declared as a `protected_column` set in the repo, and CI asserts that for every column of that table OUTSIDE the declared set, `has_column_privilege('authenticated', tbl, col, 'UPDATE')` is true — the exact assertion that would have caught gender/gender_set. A second CI grep fails any migration that computes a grant from `information_schema` at apply time.

MINOR 12 (verification never exercises the Realtime RLS evaluation path) — PARTLY closed, honestly. A local test subscribes N synthetic clients to a private topic against the local stack and measures join latency and timeout rate under a write loop, because a pool-bound join TIMES OUT rather than returning `too_many_joins`. What it cannot reproduce is hosted Realtime's own `DB_POOL_SIZE=5` default and its cluster behaviour; raising `DB_POOL_SIZE` to ≥20 is recorded as a dashboard prerequisite and as a conformance expectation read through the Management API, and the residual is stated in accepted limits rather than papered over.

MINOR 13 (prod password in CI; the 24 h `call_invites` sweep destroys the only record a call happened) — CLOSED both ways. (a) Conformance runs inside the database; the nightly structural diff uses a dedicated role with catalogue SELECT only and no data access, and never `app_secrets`. (b) The 24 h `call_invites` entry is not shipped. calling.md replaces `call_invites` with `calls` — one row per attempt with a terminal `end_reason` — which IS the call history; `call_signals` (the SDP log) is daily-partitioned and dropped at 2 days with payloads NULLed at `end_call`. The interim entry, before calling S3 lands, nulls `offer_sdp` at 10 minutes and KEEPS the row.

CROSS-DOMAIN CONTRADICTIONS I OWN AND RESOLVE. (i) `design/data.md`'s presence index is deleted; presence.md's `presence_session` is adopted. (ii) `supabase/migrations` and `staging` appear zero times across six revised siblings that are all written as staged, revertible, CI-verified plans; D0–D2 is that prerequisite, and the sibling stages it gates are listed explicitly in Migration. (iii) Bucket privacy is owned here, not deferred. (iv) The cost model is reconciled to messaging.md's. (v) The grant landmine gets a named CI assertion. (vi) Retention no longer keys off client-writable columns. (vii) THREE per-couple allocators were specified independently — transport's `couple_cursor.next_pos`, messaging's `chat_streams.last_seq`, push's `couple_seq.last_seq`. Three row locks per write is three serialization points and three integers that diverge; worse, `messages.cseq` is claimed by two of them with incompatible density rules. Resolved here as ONE physical allocator row per couple. (viii) `push_outbox` and the device table are each specified twice with different columns (push.md vs calling.md); resolved to one of each. (ix) transport's `app_config` and presence's `app_runtime_config` are one table.

===============================================================
1. THE ONE MECHANISM
===============================================================
**Every fact this app's safety depends on is declared in the repo, and the live database is interrogated for it on a schedule that does not depend on anyone remembering. A difference is an open alert with a name, a severity and an age.**

Two independent checks, sharing no code and no credential:

- **STRUCTURE.** `supabase/migrations/` is the sole source of DDL. `supabase db reset` rebuilds the database from empty; `supabase db diff --linked` must print nothing — against staging on every PR, against production nightly through a catalogue-only read-only role.
- **CONFORMANCE.** `conformance_check()` re-derives every fact that is a ROW or a SETTING rather than a schema object, and compares it to `ops_expectation`. It runs in CI against the freshly-built local database, and — this is the load-bearing part — *inside production* every 60 seconds, needing no external credential.

`db diff` compares schema. It does not compare data. The three most safety-critical runtime facts in this app are data: whether `storage.buckets.public` is true, whether the drain cron job is `active`, whether a `retention_policy` row is `enabled`. Any design that calls those "diffed" is asserting something the tool does not do. Splitting the check is the whole of fatal flaw 1.

Everything else in this document — staging, rollback, DR, RLS conformance, retention, lifecycle — is downstream of those two checks existing.

===============================================================
2. MIGRATION DISCIPLINE AND THE LEDGER
===============================================================
Committed: `supabase/config.toml` (with the CLI version pinned — an unpinned CLI silently changes what `db diff` considers a difference), `supabase/migrations/<timestamp>_<name>.sql`, `supabase/seed.sql`, `supabase/expectations/*.sql` (the conformance seed), `docs/db-census/<date>.md`.

**The baseline is captured, then PROVEN, then adopted — in that order.** `supabase db dump --schema public` from production becomes `00000000000000_baseline.sql`. It is replayed on a fresh staging project and amended freely until it replays clean, because at that point it is applied nowhere. Only then does `supabase migration repair --status applied 00000000000000` mark production's ledger, executing no DDL against production. Reversing those two steps — repairing before replaying — is a big bang in disguise: the append-only rule then forbids fixing a baseline that production already claims to have applied.

`auth` and `storage` are NOT in the baseline. The local stack runs gotrue's and storage-api's own migrations first, so an `auth`/`storage` dump collides on duplicate objects and on `ALTER ... OWNER TO supabase_admin` (`postgres` is not superuser on a Supabase project). Worse, once `storage` is in diff scope every Supabase-side storage-api upgrade produces a non-empty nightly diff for a reason nobody controls, and the alarm gets muted. `storage.buckets` rows and `storage.objects` policies are conformance expectations instead.

Rules, each with the failure it prevents:
- **Append-only, never edited after applying.** Enforced by `schema_migration_checksum(version, sha256, applied_at, applied_by)`, written by the migration wrapper. `supabase_migrations.schema_migrations` carries no checksum (unlike Flyway), so without this side table the ledger and the repo can diverge silently.
- **No `if not exists` / `do $$ ... end $$` guards.** The ledger records what ran; a migration that is a no-op on re-run is a migration whose effect you cannot verify. This is exactly why nobody can say what the three grant-rebuild blocks actually granted.
- **No environment literal.** CI greps `supabase/migrations/` and fails on a project ref, an `https://` host, a key-shaped string, or `information_schema` appearing inside a `grant`/`execute format` (the landmine idiom).
- **Every migration that changes a conformance-relevant fact must insert or update its `ops_expectation` row in the same migration.** CI fails a migration that creates a bucket, a cron job, a partitioned table or a retention entry without one. The expectation is not documentation; it is the thing that makes the change detectable when someone undoes it by hand.

The 42 historical files move to `docs/history/sql-archive/` with a README stating they are historical and must never be run.

4. THE RLS PATTERN — CORRECT AND FAST, ASSERTED NOT REVIEWED
===============================================================
One shape, applied mechanically, four policies per couple-scoped table, USING and WITH CHECK always stated separately:

  `for select to authenticated using ( couple_id = (select public.current_user_couple_id()) )`

Four documented deltas, all applied (research/supabase.md:310, the Supabase RLS performance doc): index the policy column 171 ms → <0.1 ms; wrap the auth call so it hoists to an InitPlan evaluated once per statement instead of once per row 179 ms → 9 ms and 178,000 ms → 12 ms; `TO authenticated` so the policy is not evaluated for anon at all 170 ms → <0.1 ms; SECURITY DEFINER helper to avoid the join 11,000 ms → 7 ms. Today, verified: exactly 3 policies in the whole repo carry a role list and all 3 are the `storage.objects` policies in `capsules.sql`; ZERO use the `(select ...)` wrapper. `current_user_couple_id()` stays STABLE SECURITY DEFINER and gains `parallel safe`.

`FOR ALL USING (...)` with no WITH CHECK is banned — Postgres reuses USING as WITH CHECK, which is how `20260627_call_invites.sql:26` lets a member INSERT a row naming an arbitrary caller_id.

**The assertions are ordered so none of them is vacuous.** The previous version quantified over `pg_policies`, so a table with RLS off or zero policies passed all four — and the population at risk was exactly the five tables with no DDL. Corrected order:
1. Over `pg_class`: every table in `public` has `relrowsecurity = true` AND at least one policy, except rows in `ops_rule_exemption` with a `reason` and an `expires_at`.
2. Over `pg_policies`: `roles <> '{public}'`; no bare `auth.uid()` or `current_user_couple_id()` outside a `(select ...)`; `with_check is not null` for cmd in (ALL, INSERT, UPDATE).
3. Over `pg_indexes`: an index exists on every column named in a policy predicate — subject to §6's HOT rule, which can veto an index and force the predicate to change instead.

**`realtime.messages` RLS is part of this pattern and is written here.** Verified by grep: zero `realtime.messages` policies exist in any file and no channel anywhere uses `private: true`, so today anyone with the shipped anon key and a couple UUID can join `call:<uuid>` and inject `hangup` or `offer`, or read `capsule_proximity:<uuid>` GPS every 4 s. Transport (Stage 1a), presence (Stage C) and crypto all specify their own policy set on this one table; they land as ONE migration using the shape above, with the same conformance assertions, and the topic vocabulary owned by those domains. `DB_POOL_SIZE ≥ 20` is a recorded dashboard prerequisite, not an assumption — Realtime evaluates private-channel joins on its own 5-connection pool by default and a pool-bound join times out rather than being refused.

===============================================================
5. PRIVILEGES — AN EXPLICIT MANIFEST, NOT PLATFORM DEFAULTS
===============================================================
The repo has no table-level GRANT or REVOKE anywhere, so `authenticated` holds Supabase's default full INSERT/UPDATE on every table. Three consequences, all live today: a client can write `messages.created_at`; a client can write any column no trigger overwrites; and a column-level revoke is a no-op while the table-level grant stands (which the repo's own comments already record).

Stage D4 declares `table_privilege(table_name, role, verbs[])` and `protected_column(table_name, column_name, verbs[], reason)` as committed data, applies them by migration, and asserts them by conformance. Rules:
- Every server-stamped timestamp (`created_at` on `messages`, `call_invites`, `reach_events`, `care_nudges`, and `account_deletions.requested_at`/`purge_after`) is INSERT/UPDATE-revoked from `authenticated` and stamped by a BEFORE trigger. A DEFAULT is not a guarantee; a trigger is.
- **The `profiles` column ACL is dropped entirely and table UPDATE re-granted.** `guard_couple_id` (SECURITY INVOKER, so `current_user` is the real writer) already refuses the couple_id transition outright, including from a SECURITY DEFINER caller running as owner. Keeping the ACL keeps the landmine; the trigger is the actual enforcement.
- Column ACLs survive only where no trigger equivalent exists (`capsules.unlocked_at`, `vault_pin.pin_hash`), declared in `protected_column`. CI asserts that for every column of such a table NOT in the declared set, `has_column_privilege('authenticated', tbl, col, 'UPDATE')` is true. That single assertion is what would have caught gender/gender_set.
- A grant is never computed from `information_schema` at apply time. CI greps for it.

Before D4 ships, `grep` the client for any insert naming a revoked column; PostgREST inserts that omit the column are unaffected, one that names it fails. That grep is a gate on the stage, not a hope.

===============================================================
6. INDEXING — AND THE RULE THAT VETOES AN INDEX
===============================================================
One index per named query, added in the migration that needs it, with the query named in the migration comment.

- `profiles(couple_id)` — backs `profiles_select_self_or_partner` and the partner lookup that runs on every token refresh and app resume. Missing today. `couple_id` is stable, so HOT survives.
- `messages(couple_id, cseq)` — the catch-up range scan, prunes perfectly on messaging.md's hash partition key.
- `call_invites(couple_id, created_at desc)` interim; superseded by calling.md's `calls` indexes.
- **`presence` gets NOTHING.** The v1 index `presence(couple_id, updated_at desc)` was fatal: `presence_server_time.sql` sets `new.updated_at := now()` in a BEFORE trigger on EVERY write, at ~18 writes/min/user, on a table carrying `replica identity full`. An index containing `updated_at` means no update can ever be HOT — an index insert plus a dead index tuple per heartbeat, ~3,000/s at 10k users against 250 baseline IOPS, to serve a sort over 2–3 rows per couple that costs nothing. And the table is being retired anyway: presence.md replaces it with `presence_session(user_id pk, couple_id, last_confirmed_at)`, whose entire point is that no indexed column is ever updated.

**The general rule, because "don't index presence" is a fact and this needs to be a rule.** Every table declares `table_write_profile(table_name, hot_columns[])` — the columns updated in steady state. CI fails if any index on that table includes a hot column. Hot-update tables get `fillfactor` (70 on `couple_cursor`, 80 on `presence_session`). And because a declaration can be wrong, the runtime backstop is an SLI: `n_tup_hot_upd / n_tup_upd` per table from `pg_stat_user_tables`, floor 0.9, alarmed. Its sensitivity is proven by a negative test that adds the bad index and asserts the ratio collapses.

===============================================================
7. ONE ALLOCATOR, ONE OUTBOX, ONE CONFIG — NAMESPACE RECONCILIATION
===============================================================
This domain owns the schema namespace, and three siblings independently specified overlapping objects. Unreconciled, they are not three designs; they are one broken schema.

**(a) One per-couple allocator.** transport's `couple_cursor(couple_id, next_pos)`, messaging's `chat_streams(couple_id, last_seq)` and push's `couple_seq(couple_id, last_seq)` are the SAME row under three names. Three counters means three row locks taken per write (three serialization points, and a deadlock surface the moment two are taken in different orders), and it means `messages.cseq` is claimed by two allocators with incompatible density rules. Physical shape:

  `couple_cursor(couple_id uuid pk, next_pos bigint not null default 0, chat_last_pos bigint not null default 0)`, fillfactor 70.

Allocation is `UPDATE couple_cursor SET next_pos = next_pos + 1 [, chat_last_pos = next_pos + 1 when the stream is chat] WHERE couple_id = ? RETURNING next_pos`, inside the appending row's own transaction, from a BEFORE INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE trigger on each streamed entity table. The row lock is held to COMMIT, so allocation order IS commit order and `WHERE couple_id = ? AND cseq > cursor` cannot skip a row. This is the property `messages.seq` (one global `nextval`, `receipts_v2.sql:31`) does not have and cannot be given. The entity column is named `cseq` (two of three siblings already call it that); `couple_stream.pos` is the same value; transport's `stream_pos` is a rename with no semantic change. `chat_last_pos` exists so messaging's `has_more = stream_last_seq > covered_through` stays a single-row read rather than a MAX over a partitioned table; other streams derive their head from `max(pos)` on the `couple_stream` PK index.

CI asserts: exactly one allocator table exists; every table in the stream vocabulary has an append trigger and a `NOT NULL cseq`; no function body takes two different per-couple counter locks.

**(b) One `push_outbox`, one device table.** push.md's shapes win (`push_outbox` keyed on `dedupe_key` with `watermark`, `push_devices` unique on `(user_id, token_hash)`); calling.md's needs fold in as columns and constraints on those tables (`deadline_at`, the priority/class CHECK) rather than as `device_tokens` and a second `push_outbox`.

**(c) One runtime config.** transport's `app_config` and presence's `app_runtime_config` are one table: `app_runtime_config`, one row per couple plus one global row, read over plain HTTP so it works with a dead socket. It is the revert lever every client-side stage in every domain depends on.

===============================================================
8. RETENTION — DATA, NOT CODE, WITH AN ENABLE GATE
===============================================================
`retention_policy(table_name, mode, ts_column, keep_interval, batch_size, index_name, enabled, notes)`, `mode ∈ {delete_batched, partition_drop}`.

**An entry cannot be enabled unless it passes a gate, asserted in CI and re-asserted by the sweeper at runtime:**
- `mode='delete_batched'` requires (i) `ts_column` stamped by a BEFORE trigger — joined against `pg_trigger`; (ii) `ts_column` INSERT/UPDATE-revoked from `authenticated` — joined against `information_schema.column_privileges`; (iii) a btree index whose LEADING column is `ts_column` — joined against `pg_index`. Fail any of the three and the sweeper writes a `page`-severity `ops_event` and processes nothing. Without (i) and (ii), a modified client inserts `created_at = now() + interval '10 years'` and the row is immortal — which is precisely the bug the SDP-nulling entry was added to fix. Without (iii), a "bounded" delete is a sequential scan per batch, N batches per run, every hour, on 250 baseline IOPS: an outage with a LIMIT clause on it.
- `mode='partition_drop'` requires a RANGE-partitioned table on a server-stamped key, and never issues a DELETE.

Where a monotone server integer exists, retention keys off `cseq`, not a timestamp.

Initial entries, and what is deliberately NOT swept:
- `reach_pulses` 1 h, `breath_events` 1 d — folded in from the two existing standalone cron jobs, which are then unscheduled.
- `net._http_response` 6 h explicitly (the `pg_net.ttl` default is a setting we cannot migrate, only assert), `cron.job_run_details` 7 d (Supabase documents no retention; it grows forever on a 500 MB database).
- `push_outbox` / `push_receipts` / `call_signals` / `call_events` / `ops_event`: `partition_drop`, owned by their domains, registered here so the runway alarm covers them.
- `call_invites` interim: NULL `offer_sdp` at 10 minutes (it is the bulk of the row and worthless after that, and a stale invite must stop being answerable) but **KEEP the row**. The v1 24 h purge destroyed the only record a call was attempted — there is no call-history table among the 35 and `answered_at` is declared and never written. Once calling.md S3 lands, `calls` is the history table with a terminal `end_reason` and is retained 12 months.
- **`messages`: no retention, ever.** Chat history is the product. Partitioning is HASH on `couple_id` (32 partitions, messaging.md Stage 1), which is a locality decision, not a retention mechanism — DROP PARTITION does not apply and never will. The v1 line "partition messages monthly on created_at at 100k users" is withdrawn on both counts.
- `retention='ephemeral'` Closer content: a counting view only, for 7 days, then a product decision (open decision 4). Never coupled to the safe entries.

**Storage orphan sweep.** Objects with no referencing row — flung GIFs, superseded `chat-bg` uploads, touch reaction images — are reaped by a registered job. Pre-crypto it matches on the `<couple_id>/` prefix; post-crypto it joins `storage_ledger` (crypto.md), because the object path becomes 32 random bytes with no couple id in it. Both shapes are specified so the sweep does not silently stop working at crypto's cutover.

===============================================================
9. THE OPERATIONS TICK — TWO CRON JOBS FOR THE WHOLE PROJECT
===============================================================
Supabase recommends ≤8 concurrent jobs, ≤10 min each. Six domains each wanting their own job spends that budget before anyone counts it, and the budget is also the horizontal-scaling ceiling push.md identifies at ~150k users. Allocation, decided here:

- **`push_drain`** — 10 s. Claim + lease sweep + its own partition maintenance, one invocation, push domain.
- **`ops_tick`** — 60 s. Everything else.

That leaves 6 of 8. `miles_breath_cleanup` and `miles_reach_cleanup` are unscheduled and folded into the registry.

`ops_tick` takes a non-blocking advisory lock (a still-running tick is skipped, never queued), reads `ops_job(name, schedule_seconds, next_due_at, budget_ms, enabled, last_ok, last_error)`, and runs each due job in its own subtransaction under its own wall-clock budget and `statement_timeout`, so one job's failure cannot prevent the others and a killed tick resumes rather than rolls back. Jobs: conformance evaluation, retention batches, partition create-ahead and drop, SLI evaluation, alert open/close, storage orphan sweep, crypto's purge cycle, account-deletion purge batches, export jobs.

**Partition runway is a first-class alarm nobody else raised.** push, calling, crypto and `realtime.messages` all depend on daily partitions. If the creation job dies, INSERTs fail — and the symptom is "chat is broken", with the cause three layers away. `ops_tick` creates partitions 7 days ahead and alarms when any registered partitioned table has fewer than 3 days of runway.

===============================================================
10. OBSERVABILITY — WHAT TO MEASURE SO A FAILURE CANNOT BE INVISIBLE
===============================================================
`ops_event(ts, kind, severity, couple_id, detail jsonb)` — append-only, daily-partitioned, 30 days. Rule: any path that swallows an error writes an `ops_event` first.
`ops_alert(kind, key, opened_at, closed_at, severity, detail)` — opened and closed by `ops_tick` by diffing `conformance_check()` and the SLI evaluator against currently-open alerts, which yields MTTR for free.
`ops_expectation(kind, key, expected jsonb, severity, reason)` and `ops_rule_exemption(kind, key, reason, expires_at)` — an exemption whose `expires_at` passes becomes a violation, so "temporary" exemptions cannot become permanent by silence.

**The rule that makes the metrics honest, generalised past push:** every asynchronous effect owns two rows — an *obligation* row written in the same transaction as its cause, and an *evidence* row written by the party that actually did the work. A proxy's HTTP status is neither. `pg_net` returning 200, or an edge function returning 200, proves the function was reached and nothing else. No state transition may be keyed on it. `net._http_response` is a bounded diagnostic, never evidence.

Measured, all plain SQL, no vendor:
1. Open `ops_alert` count and max age, by kind and severity — conformance violations are a number, not a memory.
2. `p50/p95(push_receipts.received_at − push_outbox.fcm_accepted_at)` and **`accepted_not_delivered`** (accepted > N minutes ago with no receipt), segmented by `Build.MANUFACTURER`. This, not `state='dead'`, is the delivery health metric; `UNREGISTERED` deletes the device row and never dead-letters.
3. `p95(claim_time − created_at)` on `push_outbox`, plus oldest pending age — the pure-SQL alarm that depends on no HTTP.
4. Retention `rows_eligible` vs `rows_deleted` per entry per run. Eligible is probed as `count(*) from (select 1 ... limit 100000) x` so the metric is cheap. Eligible > deleted for 24 h is a page: it is the difference between finding a backlog in a day and finding it in three weeks when writes fail.
5. HOT ratio `n_tup_hot_upd / n_tup_upd` per hot table, and dead-tuple ratio. The cheapest early warning for the entire class of index mistake that fatal 4 was.
6. `cron.job_run_details`: failures in 24 h and last-success age per job.
7. Partition runway per registered table.
8. `pg_stat_statements` top 10 by `total_exec_time` — where an RLS regression appears — with an alarm on a NEW entrant, not on an absolute threshold.
9. Database size against the tier limit, per-table growth per week, storage bytes per bucket, WAL generation rate.
10. Realtime reports: connected clients, joins/sec, message rate (available on all plans).
11. Ledger integrity: `schema_migration_checksum` mismatches, and applied-versions-not-in-repo.

**Alarm routing, three legs, because the first two share a fate.** (a) `ops_tick` opens alerts inside the database. (b) A single `ops-health` endpoint returns 500 when any `page`-severity alert is open OR the last `ops_tick` finished more than 5 minutes ago — shared with push.md's `push-health`, one endpoint, one pinger. (c) An external uptime pinger alarms on **silence**, which is the only leg that survives total project death and the only leg that can detect `ops_tick` itself being unscheduled. A check cannot detect its own absence; only an outside observer expecting a heartbeat can.

===============================================================
11. STORAGE AS SCHEMA
===============================================================
All four buckets become migration-defined rows — `couple_media`, `couple_intimate`, `chat-bg`, `capsule-media` — with `public = false`, `file_size_limit` and `allowed_mime_types`, plus their `storage.objects` policies. Today only `capsule-media` has DDL. Conformance asserts the flags every 60 s inside production, which is the only reason a 2am re-publicise is survivable.

Closer photos leave `bytea`. Verified: ImagePicker `imageQuality: 85`, no `maxWidth`/`maxHeight`, full-resolution JPEGs into `vault_items`, `memory_threads` and `afterglow_entries`, fetched with `.select()` (all columns, no pagination) on every screen open, and shipped hex/base64 by PostgREST at roughly 2x on the wire. At ~2 MB each, about 250 photos fills the entire Free 500 MB database — after which writes fail while Realtime keeps delivering, so the app looks alive and silently discards every write. Columns hold a path; the bytes go to Storage.

**The cutover has no public fallback window.** v1 kept the old public bucket readable for 30 days, which is a month of world-readable intimate media advertised as the fix. Instead: a data migration normalises every stored `getPublicUrl` string to an object path (`presence.checkin_photo_url`, `profiles.chat_bg_image_url`, `personal_vault_items.content`, everything consuming `messages.image_path`), the client ships a release that resolves paths to signed URLs, adoption is observed, then the flag flips once.

**Stated plainly: this is defence in depth, not a root-cause fix.** The couple UUID leaks through invite codes, any URL a user has shared, an ex-partner's memory, and CDN entries which Supabase's own Smart CDN documentation says revocation does not purge. Exposure after the flip is bounded by CDN TTL (~1 h default, invalidation up to 60 s) for objects already fetched, and unbounded for a URL someone saved. The fix that actually closes it is crypto.md's: ciphertext under a per-object DEK at a 32-random-byte path, after which a public bucket, a leaked signed URL and a stale CDN entry are all equivalent to holding random bytes. This domain's job is to make the bucket private now, cheaply, and to say out loud that it is not the end of the story.

===============================================================
12. ACCOUNT LIFECYCLE — DELETION, CANCEL, EXPORT, DISSOLUTION
===============================================================
`data_subject_map(table_name, owner_column, scope ∈ {user, couple, shared}, export bool)` drives export and purge from one declaration, so they cannot disagree. **Sourced from ALL tables in `public`**, not RLS-enabled ones — a table with RLS off must not escape the security check and the export/deletion check at the same time. A table absent from the map with no exemption fails CI. Today the deletion RPC enumerates buckets by hand (`account_deletion.sql` lists `couple_media`, `couple_intimate`, `capsule-media`) and misses `chat-bg` entirely.

`account_deletions(user_id pk, requested_at, state, purge_after, completed_at, cancelled_at, error)`, `state ∈ {requested, cancelled, purging, done, error}`.

`delete_my_account()` does three fast things and returns: write the request row (server-stamped, column-revoked), delete the user's `auth.sessions` rows (closing the documented window where the JWT stays cryptographically valid ~1 h after the row is gone), set `purge_after = requested_at + 7 days`. The physical purge is a registered `ops_tick` job in bounded batches — retryable and countable, instead of one unbatched multi-table-plus-storage delete inside a request transaction with `exception when others then null` around the storage half.

**Revocation is one function; cancellation deliberately bypasses it.** `current_user_couple_id()` returns NULL when the caller has a pending deletion or their couple is dissolved, so ~35 tables go dark atomically and a table added next month inherits it. But that leaves the user in an empty shell for seven days. `cancel_my_deletion()` and `account_state()` are SECURITY DEFINER RPCs that do NOT route through the couple helper, so the shell renders "Your account is scheduled for deletion on <date>" with a working Cancel. Without this, a user who rage-taps delete and is talked round the same evening reinstalls into a blank app with no undo, and the purge still fires on day 7.

**Couple-scoped rows survive until every member is purged.** `scope` is decided per table and committed BEFORE the purge job is enabled — shipping an irreversible purge whose semantics a verification step describes as "whichever behaviour is chosen" is not a plan. The partner is notified at `requested` (not at `done`) via a durable `couple_notice` row returned by their next bootstrap, so the notification does not depend on push delivery. Two simultaneous requests are ordered by `purge_after` and each purge re-reads the other's state, so one member's grace cannot be consumed by the other's purge.

Couple dissolution writes `couples.dissolved_at`; a registry entry purges couple-scoped rows 90 days later. Because the helper already returns NULL for a dissolved couple, that purge is storage reclamation, not a privacy race. Today `couples.active = false` is written and read by nothing.

`export_my_data()` is a request row too: an `ops_tick` job writes JSON to a private `exports` bucket and returns a signed URL, because the Edge Function CPU limit is 2 s per request and a full export will not fit in it.

===============================================================
13. BACKUP AND RESTORE
===============================================================
Free: daily logical backup, 7-day retention, no PITR. The restore rehearsal is monthly and is the DR drill and the migration test in one command: restore last night's production backup into a scratch project, apply pending migrations, run the whole verification suite, record RTO. Nobody has ever asked whether the backup restores; today a restore would not help anyway, because you would be restoring rows into a schema the repo cannot reproduce.

**Two honest complications, both named rather than discovered.** Storage and Postgres restore to different points — Storage overwrites are not carried in Postgres backups (crypto.md says so about its own overwrite-then-purge flow), so a restore can produce ledger rows whose objects are gone and objects whose ledger rows are gone. The rehearsal therefore ends with a reconciliation pass: orphan sweep plus a count of dangling references, reported. And the rehearsal is feasible on a scratch Free project only while the database is small; §14 states where that stops being true.

===============================================================
14. WHY THIS MIRRORS THE TOP TIER, AND WHERE IT DIFFERS
===============================================================
**Migration-as-ledger plus drift detection** (Flyway, Sqitch, Atlas; the Supabase CLI already implements `supabase_migrations.schema_migrations` and `db diff --linked`). Differs in two load-bearing ways: the baseline is *captured* from production by `db dump`, not reconstructed from the 42 files — reconstruction feels like the rigorous option and produces a fiction, and here it provably would, since five tables have no DDL to reconstruct from. And a checksum side table, because the Supabase ledger has none and append-only would otherwise be hoped for rather than enforced.

**Configuration conformance / desired-state reconciliation** (the Kubernetes controller loop; AWS Config rules; Chef audit mode). This is the piece the v1 design lacked entirely and it is the answer to fatal 1: a periodic re-derivation of observed state from the live system, compared to declared state, emitting a named violation. Differs deliberately: no controller, no auto-remediation. It detects and alarms; it does not reconcile, because auto-reverting a human's emergency dashboard change at 2am is how you turn an incident into an outage.

**Transactional outbox** (Richardson; Debezium's outbox router; Stripe/Shopify webhook egress). Adopted by push.md, not re-specified here. Differs from the textbook: no CDC, no Kafka — and critically, the outbox is the retry owner while the immediate wake stays on the critical path, because the textbook poll interval is fatal to a 45 s ring.

**pg_partman's retention-config-table pattern** (one config row per table, one worker). Adopted in shape. Differs: no pg_partman, and the registry carries an enable gate the original does not have, because the original assumes the timestamp is trustworthy and here it is client-writable.

**Soft-delete-then-purge with immediate revocation**, the standard GDPR shape, and notably the shape Supabase's own `auth.admin.deleteUser` cannot give you because it is synchronous. Differs: revocation lives inside the tenancy helper rather than as a `deleted_at` predicate repeated on 35 tables, which is what makes it atomic and what makes table 36 inherit it — plus an explicit cancel path reachable from the revoked state, which most implementations of this pattern omit and which is the single most likely real-world use.

**Where this deliberately builds nothing:** no Datadog, no Grafana, no external APM. Every SLI is a SELECT, every alert is a row, and the only outside dependency is a free uptime pinger whose entire job is to notice silence.

===============================================================
15. REJECTED ALTERNATIVES
===============================================================
- **Hand-reconstruct the baseline from the 42 files.** You cannot prove the reconstruction matches production, and here it provably would not: five tables have no DDL, one of them menstrual-cycle history. `db dump` gives the truth in thirty seconds.
- **Keep `db diff` as the only check and add "don't touch the dashboard" as a policy.** This is what v1 did. It cannot see a row, a flag, or a setting, which is where the three worst failure modes live.
- **Auto-remediate conformance violations.** Rejected: reverting an emergency change without knowing why it was made converts an incident into an outage. Detect, alarm, MTTR.
- **Declarative schema (Atlas, Supabase's `supabase/schemas` mode).** Genuinely attractive; rejected as the FIRST move because generated diffs need a reviewer who can spot a destructive one, and a single maintainer with no staging and no CI makes an auto-generated `drop column` a live hazard. A migrations directory is a prerequisite either way, so revisiting after D5 costs nothing.
- **JWT custom claim for `couple_id` instead of the SECURITY DEFINER helper.** Faster; rejected as the first move because it introduces a staleness window on pairing/unpairing — the exact transition this app already gets wrong — and forfeits the revocation invariant, since a claim baked into issued tokens cannot be revoked by editing one function. Revisit only if `profiles` lookups appear in `pg_stat_statements`' top 10.
- **Per-table pg_cron retention jobs.** Rejected: ~10 tables need retention today, the documented guidance is ≤8 concurrent jobs, and the budget is also the horizontal-scaling ceiling. One registry-driven dispatcher.
- **Keep `pg_net` direct and add a retry cron over `net._http_response`.** Rejected: the evidence is deleted after 6 hours by pg_net's own TTL. You cannot retry what you cannot see.
- **Buy PITR immediately.** Rejected at 1k users: PITR protects against a bad write; the actual DR gap is that the schema cannot be rebuilt, which PITR does not address at all. Buy it at 10k.
- **A separate migrations tool (Flyway, Sqitch, dbmate).** Rejected: two sources of truth about what has been applied is the disease under treatment.
- **Row-level `deleted_at` filters on every table for account deletion.** Rejected: 35 places to remember versus 1, and every future table silently opts out.
- **Give CI the production database password for the nightly diff.** Rejected: it extends the blast radius of a compromised runner or a malicious npm dependency to `app_secrets` and every message. Catalogue-only read-only role, and conformance runs inside the database instead.

## Invariants
- THE REPO PRODUCES THE DATABASE'S SCHEMA, OR CI FAILS. `supabase db reset` builds every migration against an empty Postgres; `supabase db diff --linked` prints nothing against staging on every PR and against production nightly. Enforced by CI, not by review. A column that exists in the database and in no migration is a build failure. Today, verified: five client-written tables (care_nudges, cycle_settings, cycle_events, love_reasons, app_secrets) and three client-written columns (reply_to_id, chat_theme_id, chat_bg_image_url) exist in zero .sql file.
- EVERY SAFETY-CRITICAL FACT THAT IS A ROW OR A SETTING IS RE-DERIVED FROM THE LIVE CATALOGUE EVERY 60 SECONDS INSIDE PRODUCTION AND COMPARED TO A CHECKED-IN EXPECTATION. `supabase db diff` compares DDL and nothing else. `storage.buckets.public`, `cron.job.active` and its schedule, `retention_policy.enabled`, `pg_class.relrowsecurity`, table and column privileges, publication membership, `current_setting('app.*')`, index presence, partition runway and exemption expiry are all data. Enforced by `conformance_check()` running from `ops_tick` with no external credential, opening a named `ops_alert`. A dashboard flip is an open alert within one minute, not never.
- A CHECK CANNOT DETECT ITS OWN ABSENCE, SO AN OUTSIDE OBSERVER ALARMS ON SILENCE. `ops-health` returns 500 when any page-severity alert is open or the last `ops_tick` finished more than 5 minutes ago; an external pinger alarms when the endpoint stops answering at all. Enforced outside the project's failure domain — this is the only leg that survives pg_cron, pg_net and Edge all being dead, and the only leg that detects `ops_tick` itself being unscheduled.
- NO STATE TRANSITION IS KEYED ON A PROXY'S HTTP RESPONSE. Every asynchronous effect owns an obligation row written in the same transaction as its cause, and an evidence row written by the party that actually did the work. pg_net is fire-and-forget and an HTTP 200 from an edge function proves the function was reached, not that the work succeeded. Enforced by construction and by a CI assertion that no trigger body treats a `net.http_post` result as an outcome. `net._http_response` is a bounded diagnostic and is never evidence; delivery is written only from `push_receipts`.
- A MIGRATION IS APPEND-ONLY, CHECKSUMMED, AND ITS EFFECT IS OBSERVABLE ON FIRST RUN. No `if not exists` or `do $$ ... end $$` guards — the ledger records what ran, so a migration that is a no-op on re-run is a migration whose effect cannot be verified. Enforced by `schema_migration_checksum(version, sha256)` written by the migration wrapper, because `supabase_migrations.schema_migrations` carries no checksum, plus a CI grep for guard idioms.
- NO ENVIRONMENT-SPECIFIC LITERAL APPEARS IN A MIGRATION, AND A NON-PRODUCTION DATABASE CANNOT PUSH TO PRODUCTION USERS. CI greps `supabase/migrations/` for project refs, `https://` hosts, key-shaped strings and `information_schema` inside a grant. Conformance asserts `app.functions_base_url` matches the host expected for `app.environment`. The push worker refuses to send when `app.environment <> 'production'` unless `app.allow_push` is true. Today `20260628_care_call_push.sql` hardcodes the production functions URL inside a trigger body, so a faithful copy in staging rings production phones.
- EVERY TABLE IN `public` HAS ROW LEVEL SECURITY ENABLED AND AT LEAST ONE POLICY, ASSERTED OVER `pg_class` BEFORE ANYTHING IS ASSERTED OVER `pg_policies`. Exceptions live in `ops_rule_exemption` with a reason and an `expires_at`, and an expired exemption is itself a violation. Enforced by a catalogue query. Quantifying over `pg_policies` first makes every policy-shape assertion vacuously true for a table with zero policies, and the population at risk is exactly the five tables with no DDL — one of which holds menstrual-cycle history and one of which holds the Cloudflare TURN credential.
- EVERY POLICY IS ROLE-SCOPED, INITPLAN-WRAPPED, INDEX-BACKED, AND STATES USING AND WITH CHECK SEPARATELY. `roles <> '{public}'`; no bare `auth.uid()` or `current_user_couple_id()` outside a `(select ...)`; `with_check is not null` for cmd in (ALL, INSERT, UPDATE); an index on every predicate column unless the HOT rule vetoes it. Enforced by a sub-second query over `pg_policies` and `pg_indexes`. Measured today: 3 of ~60 policies carry a role list and all 3 are storage.objects policies in capsules.sql; zero use the subselect wrapper; `FOR ALL USING (...)` with no WITH CHECK is how call_invites lets a member insert a row naming an arbitrary caller_id.
- NO INDEX MAY INCLUDE A COLUMN THAT IS UPDATED IN STEADY STATE. Each table declares `table_write_profile.hot_columns`; CI fails an index that intersects it; the runtime backstop is `n_tup_hot_upd / n_tup_upd` from `pg_stat_user_tables` with a 0.9 floor, proven sensitive by a negative test that adds the bad index and asserts the ratio collapses. Enforced by CI plus a standing SLI. `presence_server_time.sql` stamps `updated_at` on every write at ~18 writes/min/user, so indexing it would make every heartbeat cost an index insert plus a dead index tuple on a `replica identity full` table against 250 baseline IOPS.
- A RETENTION ENTRY CANNOT BE ENABLED UNLESS ITS TIMESTAMP IS SERVER-STAMPED, COLUMN-REVOKED FROM `authenticated`, AND INDEXED AS A LEADING COLUMN. Asserted in CI by joining `retention_policy` to `pg_trigger`, `information_schema.column_privileges` and `pg_index`, and re-asserted by the sweeper at runtime, which writes a page-severity `ops_event` and processes nothing rather than scanning. The repo contains no table-level grants at all, so Supabase's default full INSERT stands and `messages.created_at` is a client-settable DEFAULT: without this gate one future-dated row is immortal and one future-dated `call_invites` row stays answerable forever.
- EVERY PRIVILEGE IS DECLARED IN THE REPO; PLATFORM DEFAULTS ARE NEVER RELIED ON. `table_privilege` and `protected_column` are committed data applied by migration and asserted by conformance. No grant is ever computed from `information_schema` at apply time — `pg_dump` freezes the result, so the next ADD COLUMN produces a column `authenticated` cannot UPDATE, which has already caused one production dead end (gender/gender_set). For every table carrying a column ACL, CI asserts `has_column_privilege('authenticated', tbl, col, 'UPDATE')` is true for every column outside the declared protected set.
- EVERY SWEEP IS BOUNDED IN WORK, NOT ONLY IN ROWS, AND IS IDEMPOTENT. Batched deletes run under a per-job wall-clock budget and a `statement_timeout` inside their own subtransaction, so a killed tick resumes rather than rolls back and a second run deletes zero. Partitioned tables use DROP and never DELETE, because a DELETE takes locks a domain writer can block on — which would turn a maintenance job into a chat outage. Enforced by the dispatcher, and measured as `rows_eligible` versus `rows_deleted` per entry per run.
- THE WHOLE PROJECT SPENDS EXACTLY TWO OF ITS EIGHT CRON SLOTS. `push_drain` at 10 s and `ops_tick` at 60 s; every other maintenance job is a row in `ops_job` dispatched by `ops_tick`. Enforced by a conformance expectation over `cron.job` that fails on an unexpected job as well as on a missing one. The 8-job guidance is also the horizontal-scaling ceiling at ~150k users, so spending it carelessly now caps the app later.
- EVERY PARTITIONED TABLE HAS AT LEAST THREE DAYS OF FUTURE PARTITIONS, ALARMED BEFORE IT BECOMES AN INSERT FAILURE. `ops_tick` creates 7 days ahead. Enforced by a conformance expectation per registered table. Push, calling, crypto and `realtime.messages` all depend on daily partitions; a dead creation job presents as "chat is broken" with the cause three layers away.
- READABILITY IS REVOKED BY ONE FUNCTION, AND THE PATH BACK DELIBERATELY BYPASSES IT. `current_user_couple_id()` returns NULL for a pending deletion or a dissolved couple, so ~35 tables go dark atomically and a table added next month inherits it. `cancel_my_deletion()` and `account_state()` are SECURITY DEFINER and do NOT route through that helper, so they are reachable from the revoked shell. Enforced by the function signatures and by a test asserting both are callable while every couple-scoped table returns zero rows.
- EXPORT COMPLETENESS AND DELETION COMPLETENESS ARE THE SAME FACT. `data_subject_map` is sourced from ALL tables in `public`, not from RLS-enabled tables, and a table absent from it with no exemption fails CI. `scope` is committed per table before any purge job is enabled, and couple-scoped rows survive until every member is purged. Today the deletion RPC enumerates buckets by hand and misses `chat-bg` entirely.
- THERE IS EXACTLY ONE PER-COUPLE ALLOCATOR ROW, ONE `push_outbox`, ONE DEVICE TABLE AND ONE RUNTIME-CONFIG TABLE. Enforced by CI assertions that no second allocator table exists, that every streamed table has an append trigger and a NOT NULL `cseq`, and that no function takes two different per-couple counter locks. Three siblings independently specified three allocators; three row locks per write is three serialization points and a deadlock surface, and `messages.cseq` was claimed by two of them under incompatible density rules.
- A DESTRUCTIVE MIGRATION IS PRECEDED BY A COUNTING VIEW THAT RAN FOR AT LEAST SEVEN DAYS. Any migration deleting user rows ships in two parts: the count is merged and observed first, then the delete. Enforced by review plus the rule that the counting view is itself a migration with an `ops_expectation` row, so its absence is detectable. The `retention='ephemeral'` Closer sweep will destroy vault and memory-thread content that the UI has told users is saved, and the only safe way to learn how much is to count it first.

## Scale ceiling
This is an ops/schema domain, so the honest ceilings are not throughput numbers. Volume assumptions are messaging.md's, not v1's: 2 users per couple, **40 user-messages per user per day** (v1 assumed ~7/day and was off by roughly 6x), peak concurrency 8% of registered.

**1,000 users (~80 peak concurrent).** Nothing in this design is load-limited. `ops_tick` processes low thousands of rows/hour — under 1% of Nano's 250 baseline IOPS. `conformance_check()` scans ~60 tables and ~200 policies in milliseconds. The binding constraint at this size is not this design at all: **the Free tier's 500 MB database, consumed by Closer's full-resolution photos in `bytea`** (imageQuality 85, no maxWidth, on `vault_items`, `memory_threads` and `afterglow_entries`), at ~2 MB each and ~2x inflation on the PostgREST wire — about 250 photos fills it. FAILURE MODE: writes begin failing and the project goes read-only while Realtime keeps delivering broadcasts, so the app looks alive and silently discards every write — the worst shape of failure and the hardest to diagnose from a phone. Separately, messaging.md puts Free's death at ~280 users on the Realtime message quota, so Pro arrives for another domain's reasons long before this one binds. MUST-FIX BEFORE 1k: photos to Storage.

**10,000 users.** ~146M message rows/year, ~90 GB/year of disk including indexes (messaging.md). The RLS work pays off here or not at all — but note the query it pays off on has changed: presence.md deletes the 4-SELECT/min/user partner poll entirely, so the hot path this domain indexes for is the catch-up range scan on `messages(couple_id, cseq)` and the `profiles(couple_id)` partner lookup on token refresh and resume, not the poll. High-frequency event tables are all `partition_drop`, so the batched sweeper's row volume stays small; **the sweeper's wall clock is no longer this domain's ceiling because nothing large is swept by DELETE.** The real limits that appear here: `supabase db diff` against production starts taking minutes and must be nightly-only, never on the PR path; and `pg_class` carries 32 `messages` partitions plus ~5 daily-partitioned tables at 7-day runway plus 30-day retention, roughly 200 relations — fine, but it is now a number worth watching. PITR becomes mandatory here (§Cost).

**50,000 users — messaging.md's stated app ceiling, and where THIS domain's real ceiling appears.** ~730M message rows/year, ~440 GB of disk. **The binding constraint is restore time, not throughput.** A 440 GB logical restore into a fresh project is measured in hours, so the monthly rehearsal stops being an afternoon on a scratch Free project and becomes a scheduled exercise on a paid one; RTO becomes a number the owner has to accept rather than a checkbox. This is the honest ceiling of the discipline: the checks stay cheap forever, but *proving you can come back* does not. FAILURE MODE IF IGNORED: the backup is never rehearsed at the size that matters, and the first real restore is the test.

**100,000 users.** messaging.md does not support it (peak concurrency at 80% of the Realtime connection ceiling with no headroom for a synchronised reconnect). For this domain, ~1.46B rows/year and ~900 GB means a logical restore is a day, and the answer stops being a bigger cron job — it is a different backup posture (PITR plus a read replica plus physical restore), which is a spend and an architecture decision, not a schema one.

**THE CEILING ON THE DISCIPLINE ITSELF, STATED HONESTLY.** Supabase does not let you revoke DDL from the project owner, so dashboard edits cannot be prevented — only detected. What changed versus v1 is the detection latency and the surface: row and flag changes (bucket public, cron disabled, retention entry off, RLS dropped, grant changed) are caught in ≤60 s by a check running inside the database; DDL changes are caught within 24 h by the nightly diff. Given that hand-editing the dashboard is this project's documented historical behaviour, this remains the single most likely way the design decays, and it is a policy risk, not an engineering one. The second discipline ceiling is the single maintainer: every alert routes to one person, and a muted alert is indistinguishable from no alert — which is why `UNREGISTERED` was removed from the dead-letter path (a permanently-red alarm gets muted, and then the real outage is invisible too).

## Cost
All figures monthly, Supabase list prices per research/supabase.md. **v1's cost section is withdrawn on two counts and corrected here: it undercounted message volume by ~6x, and its private-bucket "delta" was double-counting.**

**WHAT THIS DOMAIN ACTUALLY COSTS, AT EVERY SCALE: near zero in money and real in engineering time.**
- Migrations, CI, conformance, retention, observability: $0 of infrastructure. GitHub Actions' free tier is 2,000 minutes/month; a migration check is `supabase db start` + apply + seed + assertions + diff, ~3 minutes per PR, ~150 minutes at 50 PRs/month.
- `ops_tick` and `push_drain` run on your own compute: <1% of Nano's 250 baseline IOPS at 1k users.
- `ops_event`/`ops_alert`/`ops_expectation`: single-digit MB at 30-day retention at any of these scales.
- External uptime pinger: free tier.

**THE ONE RECURRING LINE THAT IS REAL: staging.** Free is $0 but auto-pauses after ~7 days idle, and when it does the DNS record is withdrawn, so CI fails with "Failed host lookup" rather than a clean error — this project has hit that exact failure before. A weekly scheduled CI run or a pg_cron heartbeat keeps it warm at $0; if that proves flaky, Pro staging is **+$25/mo** and is the correct purchase, because a staging environment that is unreliable is worse than none (it trains you to ignore CI).

**PITR.** $100/mo on Pro. Daily backups with 7-day retention are included on Pro; Free gets daily backups and no PITR. **1k: do not buy.** PITR protects against a bad write; this app's actual DR gap is that the schema cannot be rebuilt, which PITR does not address at all — you would restore rows into a schema nobody can reproduce. Daily backup plus a reproducible repo plus a tested restore is strictly better value at that size. **10k: buy.** At that point a bad migration costs 10,000 people's history and the restore window matters more than the schema question, which is by then solved.

**RECONCILIATION WITH messaging.md, SINCE THE TWO COST MODELS DISAGREED.**
- v1 said "partition `messages` at 100,000 users"; messaging.md derives 1.46B rows/year and ~900 GB at 100k and partitions in its Stage 1 regardless of scale. **messaging.md is right and v1 is wrong twice**: partitioning happens at Stage 1 while the table is small (it is the only step that gets materially harder with volume), and it is HASH on `couple_id`, so DROP PARTITION is never a retention mechanism for chat. The row-count trigger is deleted from this design; it was answering a question that no longer exists.
- The v1 private-bucket delta (+$1.20 / +$12 / +$105) is **withdrawn as double-counting**. Private buckets bill as uncached at $0.09/GB versus $0.03/GB cached, but the revised media topology (crypto.md: opaque object ids resolved per user under RLS) never gets the cached rate anyway, and messaging.md's tables already price media egress at the uncached rate. There is no delta to add; there was a delta in v1 only because v1 implicitly assumed the cached baseline continued.
- The attacker's 200–400 GB/month presence-poll egress line is **correct against today and against v1** — v1 added an index specifically to make that poll fast — and is **removed by presence.md Stage F**, which deletes the 15 s `Timer.periodic` and the 800 ms refetch debounce, i.e. 4 unconditional ~25-column SELECTs/min/user. I do not re-price it; I record that v1's headline index existed to optimise a query another domain deletes.
- Database egress that remains and belongs to this domain's read paths: `presence_bootstrap` (~300 B, ~20/user/day), chat sync pages (~40 rows/user/day at ~300 B), push acks (~200 B each). At 10k users that is roughly **6–8 GB/month**, comfortably inside Pro's 250 GB and dominated by media long before it matters.

**MONEY THIS DOMAIN SAVES, WHICH IS LARGER THAN WHAT IT SPENDS.** Moving Closer photos out of `bytea` into Storage: Postgres disk is $0.125/GB-month past the 8 GB Pro allowance; object storage is $0.0213/GB-month past 100 GB — roughly 6x, before the base64/hex inflation on every PostgREST read of a full-resolution JPEG. At 10k users and ~100 MB of vault media each (~1 TB): ~$124/mo in Postgres against ~$19/mo in Storage, a **~$105/mo saving** — and at 1k users on Free it is not a saving at all, it is the difference between a working app and a read-only one.

**TOTALS FOR THIS DOMAIN'S MARGINAL SHARE:**
- **1,000 users: $0–25/mo** (staging Free or Pro). Against messaging.md's ~$31 whole-app figure. The claim that migration discipline is expensive is false for this project; the expensive thing is the current state.
- **10,000 users: ~$125/mo** ($25 staging Pro + $100 PITR), against messaging.md's ~$327 whole-app figure — so the honest all-in is ~$450, and ~$105/mo of that is *returned* by getting media out of Postgres.
- **50,000 users: ~$125–150/mo** in the same lines, plus the 440 GB disk already priced in messaging.md at ~$54 and the restore posture, which is engineering time rather than a subscription.

**The line worth stating for the owner:** the reason no staging environment exists is not budget. It is $0, it holds two seeded couples, and it is the prerequisite for the verification plan of every one of the six other revised designs.

## Migration
Ten stages. Each is independently shippable and independently revertible. **The census comes before the baseline, and the replay comes before the repair** — reversing either is the big bang v1 hid. Exactly one stage requires a coordinated client release, and it is named.

**STAGE D0 — CENSUS. Read-only. Half a day. Nothing is changed.**
Run a catalogue-only inspection of production and commit the output as `docs/db-census/<date>.md`: every table in `public` with `relrowsecurity`, policy count and policy text; every table and column privilege held by `anon` and `authenticated`; `storage.buckets` flags; `cron.job` rows; `pg_publication_tables`; extensions; `pg_settings` for the `app.*` GUCs; index list; trigger list.
*Value on its own:* for the first time somebody — and the repo — knows what `cycle_events` (menstrual-cycle history), `cycle_settings`, `love_reasons`, `care_nudges` and `app_secrets` (Cloudflare TURN key and API token) actually permit. v1 dumped these into the baseline and called them reviewed.
*Must not skip:* everything after this is a decision about what the census found.

**STAGE D0a — CLOSE WHAT THE CENSUS FOUND. Same day. One hand-applied file, deliberately.**
If any table has RLS off: enable it and add the standard policies. If `app_secrets` is readable by `anon` or `authenticated`: `revoke all` and reach it only through SECURITY DEFINER functions, or move it to Supabase Vault.
*Ordering is load-bearing:* this ships by hand, exactly like today, **before** the migration machinery exists, because it closes a live breach and must not wait for tooling — and because applying it now means the fix is *inside* the baseline taken in D1, rather than a migration racing a baseline that already claims to be applied.

**STAGE D1 — BASELINE, REPLAY, THEN REPAIR. 1–2 days. No DDL runs against production.**
1. `supabase init`; commit `config.toml` with the CLI version pinned.
2. `supabase db dump --schema public -f supabase/migrations/00000000000000_baseline.sql`. **Not `auth`, not `storage`.**
3. Create the staging project. `supabase db push` the baseline into it. **Amend the baseline freely until it replays clean** — it is applied nowhere, so there is nothing to violate.
4. Only now: `supabase migration repair --status applied 00000000000000` against production.
5. Add `schema_migration_checksum` and the migration wrapper that writes it.
6. Archive the 42 files to `docs/history/sql-archive/` with a do-not-run README.
*The bug this ordering prevents:* v1 repaired production first, so when the staging replay failed on duplicate objects the append-only rule forbade fixing the baseline. That is unrecoverable-by-its-own-rules.

**STAGE D2 — CI, SEED, LOCAL STACK. 2 days.**
`supabase db reset` from empty on every PR; `seed.sql` with deterministic UUIDs (2 couples, 4 users, ~50 messages, presence sessions, one call, one of each event); a pgTAP/assertion-SQL harness; `supabase db lint`; the literal grep (project ref, `https://` host, key-shaped string, `information_schema` inside a grant, guard idioms); `db diff --linked` against staging on every PR; nightly `db diff` against production through a **catalogue-only read-only role**, never the owner password.
*Value on its own:* **this is the blocking prerequisite every other revised design names and none of them owns.** From here, messaging Stage 1, push Stage 2, transport Stage 3, calling S3, crypto Stage 1 and presence Stage B all have somewhere to run their SQL property tests. Before here, all of them can only be rehearsed in production.

**STAGE D3 — THE CONFORMANCE LEDGER. 2 days. This is the fatal-1 fix and it ships BEFORE the things it guards.**
`ops_expectation`, `ops_rule_exemption` (with `expires_at`), `ops_event`, `ops_alert`, `ops_job`, `conformance_check()`, `ops_tick` (60 s, one cron slot), the `ops-health` endpoint, the external pinger. Seed expectations from the D0 census so the FIRST run is green against reality, then tighten one class at a time.
*Ordering is load-bearing:* every later stage inserts its expectation row in the same migration that makes its change, and CI fails a migration that adds a bucket, cron job, partitioned table or retention entry without one. Shipping D3 after D7 would mean the bucket flip has no watcher on the day it happens.
*Rollback:* drop the cron job; everything else is additive and inert.

**STAGE D4 — THE PRIVILEGE MANIFEST. 1–2 days.**
Declare `table_privilege` and `protected_column`; apply explicit GRANT/REVOKE for every table in `public`; revoke INSERT/UPDATE on every server-stamped timestamp and add the BEFORE triggers; **drop the `profiles` column ACL and re-grant table UPDATE** (the `guard_couple_id` trigger is the real enforcement and the ACL is only a landmine); keep declared column ACLs on `capsules.unlocked_at` and `vault_pin.pin_hash` with the `has_column_privilege` assertion.
*Gate:* grep the client for any insert naming a newly-revoked column before shipping. PostgREST inserts that omit the column are unaffected.
*Must land before D6:* retention on a client-writable timestamp is retention an attacker can opt out of.

**STAGE D5 — RLS SWEEP, INDEXES, AND `realtime.messages`. 2–3 days, one migration per table.**
Per table: `TO authenticated` + `(select public.current_user_couple_id())` + explicit WITH CHECK, and the index the policy needs in the SAME migration — `profiles(couple_id)` yes, **`presence` never**. Order: messages, profiles, chat_receipts first.
In ONE migration (not three): `realtime.messages` policies for the current topic vocabulary, shared with transport Stage 1a and presence Stage C, with `DB_POOL_SIZE ≥ 20` raised and recorded first. Today zero such policies exist and no channel is `private: true`, so a stranger with the shipped anon key and a couple UUID can inject `hangup` into any call and read GPS every 4 s.
Land the `pg_class`/`pg_policies` assertions as warnings, flip to hard failures when the non-conforming count reaches zero.
*Rollback:* per table, per PR.

**STAGE D6 — RETENTION REGISTRY AND PARTITION MAINTENANCE. 2 days, deliberately split.**
`retention_policy` with the enable gate; the batched sweeper and the partition create-ahead/drop jobs inside `ops_tick`; unschedule `miles_breath_cleanup` and `miles_reach_cleanup` and fold them in; prune `cron.job_run_details` and `net._http_response`. Enable only: `reach_pulses`, `breath_events`, cron history, net responses, and the `call_invites` **10-minute SDP null with the row retained**.
SEPARATELY AND LATER: a counting view for `retention='ephemeral'` Closer content, merged and observed for 7 days before anything is decided. Do not couple it to the safe entries.
*The bug this ordering prevents:* v1's 24 h `call_invites` purge destroyed the only record that a call was attempted, for an app with no call-history table.

**STAGE D7 — STORAGE AS SCHEMA. 2 days + THE ONE RELEASE-COUPLED STEP.**
Bucket rows and `storage.objects` policies as migrations with conformance expectations; the orphan sweep (prefix-matched now, `storage_ledger`-joined after crypto).
Cutover, in order: (a) data migration normalising every stored public URL to an object path; (b) client release resolving paths to signed URLs; (c) observe adoption; (d) flip `public = false`. **No 30-day public fallback** — v1's version left every historical intimate photo world-readable for a month after the change advertised as fixing it.
Separately and independently: Closer photos out of `bytea` into Storage.

**STAGE D8 — ACCOUNT LIFECYCLE. 3 days.**
`data_subject_map` over ALL tables in `public` plus the CI completeness check; the `scope` decision committed per table; `account_deletions` with `cancelled`; `delete_my_account()` becomes request + session revoke; `cancel_my_deletion()` and `account_state()` outside the couple helper; the partner `couple_notice` at `requested`; purge as a bounded `ops_tick` job; `current_user_couple_id()` returns NULL for pending deletion and dissolved couples (one function change, ~35 tables); `export_my_data()` as an async job to a private `exports` bucket.
*Ordering:* the `scope` decision and the cancel path ship BEFORE the purge job is enabled. Shipping an irreversible purge whose semantics are still open is the failure v1 committed.

**STAGE D9 — RESTORE REHEARSAL, MONTHLY, ONGOING.**
Restore last night's backup into a scratch project, apply pending migrations, run the full verification suite, run the storage/Postgres reconciliation pass, record RTO. Re-evaluate PITR against the 10k trigger.

**ORDERING RULES, EACH NAMING THE BUG IT PREVENTS.**
- D0 before D1: otherwise the baseline blesses an unknown RLS state on menstrual-cycle history and a TURN credential.
- D0a before D1: otherwise the fix races a baseline production already claims to have applied.
- D1's replay before D1's repair: otherwise a failed replay is unfixable under the append-only rule.
- D2 before messaging S1 / push S2 / transport S3 / calling S3 / crypto S1 / presence S B: otherwise those tables become files 44–48 and their verification levels have no environment.
- D3 before D6 and D7: a bucket flip or a disabled retention row with no watcher is exactly fatal 1.
- D4 before D6: retention on a client-writable timestamp is defeated by one insert.
- D5's `realtime.messages` migration is ONE migration shared by three domains: three independent policy sets on one table is a merge conflict at best and a hole at worst.
- Nothing in this plan re-enables a path an earlier stage disabled, and no stage removes a recovery mechanism before its replacement is live.
- **If the project stops after D3, the two worst properties are already fixed:** the repo describes the database, and a 2am dashboard change to a bucket, a cron job or a retention row is an alert within sixty seconds instead of never.

## Verification
GOVERNING PRINCIPLE: every claim in this domain is checkable against a Postgres container on one laptop, or one CI runner. No network, no partner, no second device, no correct clock. That is not convenience — it is the point, because the reason previous fixes passed and then failed is that the only available test was two NTP-synced phones on one wifi, which can produce none of the conditions that matter.

**SECOND GOVERNING PRINCIPLE, AND IT IS THE ONE v1 LACKED: every assertion ships with a test that BREAKS it.** An expectation never exercised in the failing direction is not an expectation; it is a comment. Each item below therefore has a negative case.

1. **REPRODUCIBILITY.** `supabase db start && supabase db reset` applies every migration to an empty database. Negative: delete a migration file and assert the build fails. This single command would have caught every "column exists in NO sql file" finding at zero cost at any point in the last year.

2. **LEDGER INTEGRITY.** Apply migrations, mutate one applied file, re-run the wrapper: assert a checksum mismatch is reported. Assert an applied version absent from the repo is reported. This is the check the Supabase ledger cannot do on its own.

3. **STRUCTURAL DRIFT.** `db diff --linked` prints nothing against staging on every PR. Negative: `ALTER TABLE` in the linked database by hand and assert the diff goes non-empty and CI fails.

4. **CONFORMANCE — THE FATAL-1 TEST, AND IT IS ENTIRELY NEGATIVE.** Against the local stack, one case per expectation kind: set `storage.buckets.public = true` → assert a `bucket` violation with the expected severity. `UPDATE cron.job SET active = false` → assert a `cron_job` violation. `UPDATE retention_policy SET enabled = false` → assert a `retention_entry` violation. `ALTER TABLE ... DISABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` → assert an `rls_enabled` violation. `REVOKE`/`GRANT` off-manifest → assert a `privilege` violation. Drop a table from the publication → assert it. Set `app.functions_base_url` to the production host in a non-production database → assert it. Let an `ops_rule_exemption` expire → assert the exemption itself becomes the violation. Then assert `ops-health` returns 500 while any page-severity alert is open, and — the one that cannot be tested from inside — stop `ops_tick` and assert the health endpoint goes stale so the external pinger's silence alarm is the thing that fires.

5. **RLS CORRECTNESS AS SQL, NOT AS A PHONE TEST.** With the seed loaded, `set request.jwt.claims` to user A of couple 1: assert `select count(*) from <t>` returns only couple 1's rows, for every table in `data_subject_map`, generated by looping the map rather than written by hand. Assert an unrelated third user gets zero everywhere. Assert `insert into call_invites (...)` naming a caller_id that is not the caller is REJECTED — that assertion FAILS against today's schema, which is the proof it is worth writing. **Negative for the vacuity bug:** create a table with RLS disabled and zero policies and assert the check FAILS; v1's four `pg_policies` assertions all passed for exactly that table.

6. **RLS PERFORMANCE, MEASURED NOT FELT.** Scale the seed with `generate_series` to ~1M messages and ~100k profiles, then `explain (analyze, buffers)` under an authenticated role: assert an Index Scan AND assert the policy predicate appears as `InitPlan 1`. An unwrapped call shows up as a per-row `Filter`; a `(select ...)`-wrapped one shows up exactly once. The defect is directly readable in EXPLAIN output and greppable in a CI assertion, with no production traffic.

7. **HOT UPDATES — THE FATAL-4 TEST, WITH ITS NEGATIVE.** Run a synthetic 10,000-iteration update loop against `presence_session` and `couple_cursor`; assert `n_tup_hot_upd / n_tup_upd > 0.95` from `pg_stat_user_tables`. Then **add the banned index and assert the ratio collapses**, which is what proves the SLI is sensitive rather than decorative. Assert CI rejects an index whose columns intersect `table_write_profile.hot_columns`.

8. **RETENTION.** Seed backdated rows via `generate_series`; call the sweeper directly (not via cron); assert exact counts before and after; assert the batch bound was respected; assert a second run deletes zero (idempotence); assert the NEGATIVE — a row inside the keep window survives; assert `rows_eligible` is reported alongside `rows_deleted`. **Enable-gate tests:** attempt to enable an entry whose `ts_column` is client-writable → refused with a page-severity `ops_event`; whose column has no BEFORE trigger → refused; whose leading-column index is missing → refused. Assert `cron.job_run_details` shrinks.

9. **PRIVILEGES — THE LANDMINE TEST.** Assert `has_column_privilege('authenticated','public.messages','created_at','INSERT')` is false, and that a raw insert naming `created_at` fails. Then the one that matters: `ALTER TABLE profiles ADD COLUMN <x>` in a migration and assert `authenticated` can UPDATE it. That assertion fails against v1's frozen column ACL and is byte-for-byte the /role-setup dead end that `newuser_fixes.sql:142-173` documents. Assert CI rejects a migration computing a grant from `information_schema`.

10. **RETENTION IMMORTALITY.** As an authenticated JWT, attempt to insert a `call_invites` row with `created_at = now() + interval '10 years'` and assert it is REJECTED. Against today's schema it succeeds and the row is immortal and permanently answerable.

11. **PARTITION RUNWAY.** Advance the test clock 7 days without running the maintenance job and assert the alarm fires BEFORE any insert fails. Then advance further and assert the insert failure, so the ordering of alarm-before-outage is proven rather than assumed.

12. **DELETION, CANCEL AND EXPORT.** Run `delete_my_account()` as seeded user A, then BEFORE the purge job runs assert: A's `auth.sessions` rows are gone; `current_user_couple_id()` returns NULL for A; every table in `data_subject_map` returns zero rows for A under A's own JWT; **`account_state()` and `cancel_my_deletion()` still work** — that is the empty-shell escape and it is the assertion v1 could not have written. Cancel, and assert full restoration. Re-request, run the purge, assert physical absence, and assert **the partner's rows are untouched** and that couple-scoped rows survive until both members are purged. Assert the partner's `couple_notice` exists from the `requested` transition, not the `done` one. Assert export output covers exactly `data_subject_map where export`.

13. **ENVIRONMENT ISOLATION.** Assert a push attempted with `app.environment <> 'production'` and `app.allow_push` unset is refused, and that conformance opens an alert when a non-production `app.functions_base_url` points at the production host. This is the check that stops staging ringing production phones.

14. **REALTIME AUTHORIZATION PATH — PARTIAL, AND SAID SO.** Subscribe N synthetic clients to a private topic against the local stack under a write loop; assert join **latency and timeout rate**, not `too_many_joins`, because a pool-bound join times out rather than being refused. Assert a stranger JWT's join is rejected. What this cannot reproduce is hosted Realtime's own `DB_POOL_SIZE=5` default and cluster behaviour; that is a conformance expectation read through the Management API and a residual limit, not a test.

15. **RESTORE REHEARSAL — MONTHLY, THE DR DRILL AND THE MIGRATION TEST IN ONE.** Restore last night's production backup into a scratch project, apply pending migrations, run items 1–13, run the storage/Postgres reconciliation (orphan objects and dangling ledger references, both counted), record RTO. This is the only thing that proves the backup restores at all; today nobody knows, because the question has never been asked.

**WHAT THIS DELIBERATELY DOES NOT VERIFY:** whether calling, messaging, presence or push work. Those belong to other domains. The claim here is narrower and much stronger — that the database the app talks to is the database in the repo, that its access rules and privileges are what they say they are, and that a change to any of that by hand becomes an alarm with a name and an age.

**FLAGGED GAP, STATED PLAINLY.** SQL test coverage today is ZERO. `mobile/test/unit` holds 20 test files and all are Dart-level; `receipts_v2_test.dart` tests the Dart model, not the `ack_read` RPC. Every check above is new work and none of it exists. Nothing in this document should be read as describing coverage that is present.

## Accepted limits
**1. Dashboard DDL cannot be prevented, only detected — and the two detection legs have different latencies.** Supabase does not let you revoke DDL from the project owner. Row and setting changes (bucket public, cron disabled, retention row off, RLS dropped, grant changed, publication membership) are caught in ≤60 s by the conformance job inside production. DDL changes are caught within 24 h by the nightly diff. *Residual risk:* up to 24 hours of undetected schema drift, and given that hand-editing the dashboard is this project's documented historical behaviour, this is the most likely way the design decays. It is a policy risk covered by an agreement, not by engineering.

**2. A check cannot detect its own absence.** If someone unschedules `ops_tick`, no internal query notices. The external dead-man's-switch pinger closes this by alarming on silence — but the pinger is a third party, and if the pinger and the project fail together the first report comes from a user. *Residual risk:* a correlated failure of the monitoring and the monitored produces exactly the invisibility being fixed, with lower probability.

**3. A material set of settings lives outside Postgres and outside migrations.** `DB_POOL_SIZE`, `pg_net.ttl`, the Pro spend cap, JWT expiry, Auth rate limits, Storage CDN behaviour, and the Realtime tenant limits are dashboard or platform settings. Some are readable through the Management API with a scoped token and become conformance expectations; the rest are a checklist a human runs. *Residual risk:* `DB_POOL_SIZE` silently reverting is a production-only failure mode this domain can name but not test, and transport, presence and crypto all depend on it being ≥20.

**4. Storage and Postgres restore to different points in time.** Storage overwrites are not carried in Postgres backups. A restore therefore produces some ledger rows whose objects are gone and some objects whose rows are gone. The rehearsal reconciles and counts both, but a deleted-then-restored object cannot be un-deleted. *Residual risk:* a point-in-time recovery is not a single consistent point across the two systems, and crypto.md's "overwrite with random bytes then purge" is irreversible in a way the Postgres restore cannot undo.

**5. `supabase db diff` is not a perfect DDL comparator.** It is migra-based and is known to be imperfect for some object classes — policy expressions, comments, certain extension state, function bodies under reformatting. Conformance covers the classes that matter for safety, but a *false-empty* diff is possible. *Residual risk:* a schema difference that neither check sees. This is exactly why conformance was not built on top of the diff.

**6. Making buckets private does not retract anything already distributed.** The couple UUID leaks through invite codes, shared URLs, an ex-partner's memory, and CDN entries which Supabase's own Smart CDN documentation says revocation does not purge. After the flip, exposure is bounded by CDN TTL (~1 h default, invalidation up to 60 s) for already-fetched objects and is unbounded for a URL someone saved. *Residual risk:* historical intimate media remains reachable by anyone who kept a link. The only thing that actually closes this is crypto.md's encryption at rest under random object paths; the bucket change is defence in depth and is not presented as anything else.

**7. `messages` retention is deliberately zero, so disk grows forever.** ~146M rows/year at 10k users, ~730M and ~440 GB at 50k. That is a product decision (chat history is the product), not an oversight, and hash partitioning on `couple_id` gives locality but no reclamation path. *Residual risk:* a permanently rising disk line and, past ~50k, a restore measured in hours.

**8. Every alarm routes to one person.** A muted alert is indistinguishable from no alert. Two mitigations are structural — severities, and a health endpoint that 500s rather than a message that can be swiped away — and one class of false alarm was removed by design (FCM `UNREGISTERED` no longer dead-letters, because every reinstall would otherwise keep the one delivery alarm permanently red until it was muted). *Residual risk:* alarm fatigue is unaddressed beyond that, and it is the most common way this kind of system dies.

**9. The privilege manifest and the write profiles are checked-in data, so they can be edited.** CI asserts the database matches the manifest; nothing asserts the manifest is right. *Residual risk:* a reviewer approving a manifest change is the last line of defence for privileges and for the HOT rule. The runtime HOT SLI is the backstop for the second; there is no backstop for the first.

**10. Append-only is enforced only for migrations applied through the wrapper.** A migration typed into the dashboard bypasses both the Supabase ledger's own recording and the checksum side table. *Residual risk:* the same class as limit 1, restated at the ledger level.

**11. Verification cannot reach hosted Realtime's authorization path.** Item 14 measures join latency and rejection against a local stack; it cannot reproduce the hosted 5-connection pool, the cluster's routing, or per-tenant limits. *Residual risk:* an RLS policy that is fast on the PostgREST path and slow in Realtime's evaluation context passes CI. Stated rather than papered over with a PostgREST EXPLAIN standing in for it.

**12. The restore rehearsal proves a logical backup restores; it proves nothing about PITR or a regional failure.** PITR is not available on Free and is not bought until 10k users, so until then the recovery point is up to 24 hours old. *Residual risk:* a bad write at 09:00 costs the day's data, and that is an accepted, priced trade at 1k users rather than a solved problem.

## Open decisions
**1. STAGING ON FREE OR PRO? — Recommend Free plus a keep-warm, upgrade the moment it costs a debugging session.** Free is $0 but auto-pauses after ~7 days idle, and when it pauses the DNS record is withdrawn, so CI fails with "Failed host lookup" rather than a clean error. This project has hit that exact failure before, on production. A weekly scheduled CI run or a pg_cron heartbeat keeps it warm at $0. The reason this is a decision and not a default is that a *flaky* staging environment is worse than none — it trains you to ignore CI, which is the one thing the whole design rests on. If the keep-warm proves unreliable within a month, pay the $25 without further discussion.

**2. WHO MAY TOUCH THE DASHBOARD SQL EDITOR? — Recommend an explicit agreement that it is SELECT-only, and that a conformance page is a Sev-2.** There is no technical control available. This needs to be a real agreement rather than an aspiration, because the entire design rests on it and the documented historical behaviour of this project is the opposite. **If that agreement will not hold, say so now**: the alternative design is materially worse but honest — accept drift, re-baseline weekly from a fresh `db dump`, and treat the repo as a record rather than a source of truth. I would rather build that than pretend.

**3. DO WE FLIP `couple_media` TO PRIVATE, AND WHEN? — Recommend yes, in D7, staged over two releases with no public fallback window.** It is the only user-visible step in the whole plan: every `getPublicUrl` string already persisted stops resolving, across `presence.checkin_photo_url`, `profiles.chat_bg_image_url`, `personal_vault_items.content` and everything consuming `messages.image_path`. Ship path-normalisation and signed-URL reading first, observe adoption, then flip once. **Do not keep the old bucket readable for 30 days** — that is a month of world-readable intimate media attached to the change advertised as fixing it. Owner's call because it is the only step users can feel, and because it should be stated to them plainly that it reduces exposure rather than ending it.

**4. DO WE DELETE `retention='ephemeral'` CLOSER CONTENT THAT THE APP PROMISED WOULD EXPIRE AND NEVER DID? — Recommend count for 7 days, then notify in-app, then sweep with a 30-day grace. Never sweep silently.** Some of these rows are years old, and the UI has told users both that this content expires and that it is saved. This is a product and trust decision wearing an engineering costume and it belongs to whoever owns the product promise, not to the retention registry.

**5. WHAT IS THE `scope` OF EACH COUPLE-SHARED TABLE ON DELETION? — Recommend couple-scoped rows survive until every member is purged, and this must be decided BEFORE D8 enables the purge.** The current RPC only deletes shared media when the last member leaves, which contradicts the dialog copy. Whichever way it is decided, the copy and the code must be made to agree in the same release, and the test pins the chosen behaviour. Shipping an irreversible purge with this open is the specific failure the previous plan committed.

**6. SHOULD `clear_conversation_everyone()` STAY A HARD DELETE? — Recommend the messaging.md shape and stop treating it as a retention question.** messaging.md already resolves it: the clear consumes a `cseq`, writes a `clear` control row carrying `clear_through_cseq`, then hard-deletes at or below it, so the partner gets an authenticated blank signal and no cursor is orphaned. The remaining product question is narrower than v1 framed it: whether "delete means delete" should be literal, or whether a support-window tombstone is wanted. **Recommend literal**, because content genuinely leaving the database is the stronger promise for this product — and note the WAL burst v1 worried about disappears anyway when `messages` leaves the publication.

**7. WHEN DO WE BUY PITR? — Recommend at 10,000 users, or at the first paying customer, whichever comes first. $100/mo.** Not before. PITR protects against a bad write; the actual DR gap today is that the schema cannot be rebuilt, which PITR does not address at all. Until then the accepted recovery point is up to 24 hours old and that is stated in accepted limits rather than hidden.

**8. HOW MUCH DOES `call_events` RETAIN? — Recommend 30 days by partition drop, subject to the owner's privacy posture.** It is the only production truth about OEM kill behaviour and it carries no content, only timestamps and device class — but it is per-call, per-device timing data in an app built around concealment. 7 days still supports the headline metric and loses only week-over-week trend. This is calling.md's decision to make; it is repeated here because the retention registry is where it becomes real.

**9. ADOPT THE DECLARATIVE SCHEMA WORKFLOW AFTER D5? — Recommend revisit then, not now.** A generated diff needs a reviewer who can spot a destructive one, and a single maintainer with no staging and no CI makes an auto-generated `drop column` a live hazard. A migrations directory is a prerequisite either way, so deferring costs nothing.

**10. WHO RECEIVES ALARMS, AND ON WHAT CHANNEL? — Needs an answer before D3 ships, and I do not have enough to recommend one.** The health endpoint and the external pinger are specified; the notification target is not. It must not be the app itself (which is the thing being monitored, and which is deliberately disguised), and it should not be an email address nobody reads at 2am. This is the smallest open item and the one most likely to quietly make the whole observability design decorative.
