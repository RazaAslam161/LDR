Miles — Security & Vulnerability Disclosure

Last updated: 4 September 2026

If you have found a way to reach data in Miles that should not be reachable, we want to hear about it, and this page is the route. It says what to send, what we consider in scope, what we do not, what we promise in return, and how long we take. Like the rest of our documents it is written to be accurate rather than reassuring: where we cannot promise something, it says so instead of implying it.

One person builds and runs Miles. There is no security team, no ticketing system and no on-call rotation. A report reaches one inbox and one human reads it. That is why the times in section 6 are stated in days rather than hours, and why they are the times we can actually keep rather than the times that would look better on this page.

1. How to report

Email milesapp.officials@gmail.com and begin the subject line with SECURITY. That word is what separates a vulnerability report from ordinary mail, and mail carrying it is read first.

A report is most useful when it contains:

  • What you found, in one or two sentences, before the detail;
  • How to reproduce it — the exact steps, requests or code that produce the result, and the accounts you used (your own; see section 5);
  • What it lets someone do that they should not be able to do. This matters more than a severity label: a finding described by its impact gets fixed faster than one described by its category;
  • Which build — the version and build number shown in Settings → About;
  • anything that would help us confirm it: a request and response pair, a log line, a short recording.

Send it in plain email. There is no PGP key, and publishing one we do not use every day would be a worse promise than saying this. If a finding is sensitive enough that you would rather not put it in an email at all, send a first message that says only that — with none of the detail in it — and we will agree a channel before you write anything down.

There is a machine-readable pointer to this page at described by RFC 9116.

2. Before you start: what Miles already says about itself

Miles publishes what it protects and what it does not, and the two halves are not the same. Reading this first will save you writing up something we have already written down.

End-to-end encrypted Memory Threads (titles, notes, partner notes, places and photographs), the text of Wish Jar entries, the reactions put on a message, and the messages and notes written during a separation. These are encrypted on the sending phone; the server stores ciphertext it holds no key for.
Not end-to-end encrypted Chat text and its photos, videos, voice notes and documents; the shared gallery; time capsules; everything in the Personal Vault — its files, its thumbnails, its notes and every item label; location; cycle data; presence; profile. These are protected by per-row access rules and private storage buckets — and the Vault additionally by its PIN and by a block on screenshots — but not from the operator of the database.

Section 2 of the Privacy Policy is the authoritative version of that table, and section 3 explains the one structural trade underneath it: a sealed copy of each phone's private key is kept on the server, wrapped with a key derived from the account holder's own password. That means the password is the whole of that protection, and we say so rather than claiming the encryption is mathematically beyond our reach.

Both of those are documented design, not defects. A report that the server can read chat, or that someone holding a user's password can open that user's encrypted history, is describing this page's own section 2 back to us.

3. What is in scope

  • The Miles Android app — the Google Play build, including anything in the shipped package itself. That is the only build anyone installs; an unshrunk debug build exists for development and is not in scope.
  • The backend the app talks to — the database, its row-level access rules, the storage buckets and signed links, the server functions the app calls, and the authentication flows behind them.
  • This site, miles-legal.vercel.app, including the account-deletion page and the one-time code it sends.

The findings we most want, stated concretely so you know what counts:

  • One couple's data reaching a third account. Anything that lets an account read, write or delete data belonging to a couple it is not part of — a missed access rule, an identifier that can be swapped, a server function that trusts a value the client sent instead of deriving it.
  • Reaching a file you should not have. Storage objects are meant to be reachable only through short-lived signed links issued to the couple that owns them.
  • Breaking the encryption we do claim. Anything that lets the server, the network, or a third party read Memory Threads, Wish Jar entry text, message reactions, or the messages and notes written during a separation. The Personal Vault is not in this list — section 2 says why, and "the operator can read the vault" is documented design rather than a finding. This includes substituting a partner's published key: each phone remembers the key it has been talking to and refuses a change until a human confirms it, so a way to make a phone accept a new key without that confirmation is exactly the class we want. One limit there is already known and conceded — the very first key a phone ever sees is trusted on sight, which is why the app also offers a safety code the two of you can read aloud to each other. "The first fetch could be a lie" is the design, not a finding.
  • Opening a sealed key without the password. The escrow blob is meant to be worthless to anyone who does not have the account password. Anything that weakens that is in scope; knowing the password is not (section 4).
  • Account takeover — sign-in, password reset, email change, session handling, or the deletion page's one-time code being used against an account that is not yours.
  • Server functions that are reachable without a token. A few are, deliberately, and each enforces its own authorisation instead. A way past one of those checks is in scope.
  • Anything in the app that leaks private content off the device — into a log, a crash report, a notification payload, a URL, a cache another app can read, or a third-party service.

4. What is out of scope

These are not bugs we can act on, and a report consisting only of one of them will get a short answer rather than a fix.

  • Social engineering. Phishing, pretexting or otherwise manipulating the developer, any Miles user, or any of the providers listed in section 4 of the Privacy Policy. Do not attempt it; it is out of scope and it is not covered by section 7.
  • Denial of service. Volumetric attacks, load testing, resource exhaustion, rate-limit flooding, or anything whose finding is "it falls over under enough traffic". Please do not generate load against the backend at all.
  • Spam and bulk automation. Mass mail, mass account creation, and raw scanner output submitted without a described impact.
  • Anything that needs a rooted, jailbroken or malware-carrying device, or physical access to an unlocked phone. This is already the app's stated threat model rather than a gap in it: section 10 of the Privacy Policy says plainly that a phone is the weakest link, and that the app lock and the vault PIN do not survive somebody who is holding the unlocked handset. Miles defends the data on its way to our servers, while it sits there, and against a third party — it does not defend against someone who already holds the unlocked device the data is decrypted on.
  • Anything that needs the account holder's password. The password opens the escrowed key by design, and the Privacy Policy says so in section 3.
  • The partner seeing what you sent them. That is the product. Miles cannot make a partner forget, and it cannot take back what has been shown.
  • The unencrypted half of section 2. That the operator of the database could read chat is documented, not discovered.
  • Third-party services. Issues in Supabase, Google (Firebase Cloud Messaging, STUN, the Android geocoder, ML Kit, and Maps when you tap Navigate), Cloudflare, Mapbox, Giphy, Vercel, an embedded video site or a news publisher the News cover reads belong to those companies and should be reported to them. Section 4 of the Privacy Policy is the full list and says what each one receives. Tell us as well if the issue reaches Miles users through us, and we will relay it.
  • Best-practice findings with no path to impact — a missing header, a permissive setting, a version number in a banner. These are read and answered, and some of them get fixed, but they are not treated as vulnerabilities and they do not get a 90-day clock.

5. Rules for testing

One rule matters more than the rest: do not touch anybody else's data. Miles exists to keep two people's private life between the two of them, and a piece of research that reads a stranger's messages has done the exact harm it was looking for.

  • Test against accounts you own. Miles pairs by an invite code one person hands the other, with no directory and no discovery — so create two accounts of your own, pair them to each other, and you have a complete couple to work against with nobody else's data anywhere in it.
  • Stop at proof. Once you can show a thing is possible, stop. Do not enumerate further, do not pivot, and do not pull more records to make the report look heavier.
  • Do not modify or destroy anything you did not create, and do not degrade the service for anyone using it.
  • If you reach somebody else's data by accident — it happens; that is often what the bug is — stop immediately, do not read further, tell us what you saw at the level of "a message body was returned" rather than quoting it, and delete every copy you hold. Say in the report that you did.
  • Keep the finding between us until it is fixed, or until the 90 days in section 6 have passed. If you plan to publish after that, tell us in the first email so nobody is surprised.

6. What we do when a report arrives

Acknowledge Within 7 days. A human reply saying we have it and whether we have understood it. If you have not heard anything in 7 days, assume the mail was lost and send it again.
Fix, or explain Within 90 days of that acknowledgement, for anything we accept as in scope. One of the two: it is fixed, or you get a written explanation of why it is not going to be — including "we cannot fix this without breaking the app, and here is what we did instead". A finding we do not intend to fix is told to you as that, not left silent.
Tell you when it ships A fix in the app reaches users only when a new build does, and we will say which build carries it. A fix on the server is usually immediate.
Credit If you want it, and the finding is real, we will name you when the fix is described. If you would rather not be named, say so and you will not be.

There is no bug bounty and no payment. Miles is a small independent app with no revenue behind a rewards programme, and offering money we could not reliably pay would be worse than saying this. Nothing on this page should be read as an offer of compensation.

7. No legal action for good-faith research

If you research Miles in good faith and within this page, we will not bring or support legal action against you for it, and we will not ask anyone else to. If a third party brings a claim about work that followed this policy, we will say publicly and in writing that the work was authorised.

Good faith means what section 5 describes: your own accounts, no harm to users, no more access than the proof needed, and telling us before telling anyone else. This promise does not cover deliberately reading other people's data, destroying or holding data to ransom, demanding payment in exchange for withholding a finding, or anything that breaks a law independently of this policy. We also cannot waive obligations that are not ours — if what you do breaks a hosting provider's terms, that is between you and them.

8. Reporting something that is not a vulnerability

Two other routes exist and are better suited than this one:

  • Abuse, harassment or a person misusing the app — report it inside the app: Settings → Support → Report a problem, or the menus in Chat and the Gallery. The Terms of Use describe what happens next.
  • Child safety — the Child Safety Standards page has its own contact and its own subject line, and reports carrying it are handled ahead of everything else.
