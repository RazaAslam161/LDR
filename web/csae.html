Miles — Child Safety Standards

Last updated: 4 September 2026

Miles is a private messaging app for two adults in a relationship. It is for adults only — you must be 18 or older to hold an account — it has no public surface, and it has no place for children in it at all. This page states our standards against child sexual abuse and exploitation (CSAE), how to report it, what we do when a report arrives, and who to contact. Like the rest of our documents it is written to be accurate rather than reassuring: where our ability to act is limited, it says so.

1. The standard

Child sexual abuse and exploitation is prohibited in Miles without exception. Specifically, you may not use Miles to create, request, send, receive, store, solicit or link to:

  • Child sexual abuse material (CSAM) — sexual content involving anyone under 18, in any form, real, drawn, animated or computer-generated. There is no exception to this and no context that changes it.
  • Sexualisation of a minor — any depiction or description of a person under 18 for sexual gratification, including material that is not itself explicit.
  • Grooming — contacting or attempting to contact a minor for sexual purposes, or building a relationship with a minor with that intent.
  • Sextortion — threatening to release a minor's intimate images, or coercing a minor into producing them.
  • Trafficking or advertising a minor for sexual purposes.

This restates and does not replace section 3 of the Terms of Use, which is the agreement you accept when you use Miles. An account used for any of the above is closed. There is no warning step for material involving minors.

Miles itself writes no sexual content. The app supplies a private, secure space between two paired adults; it does not author or generate sexual material of any kind. There is no feed, no timeline, no recommendation engine and nothing any user posts is discoverable by anyone but their partner.

Three places where the app puts content it did not write in front of someone, and none of them is a feed, a recommendation or anything another user can reach you through. (Two smaller ones, for completeness: a link posted in chat shows a preview thumbnail fetched from the linked site, and the map draws its tiles from Mapbox. Section 4 of the Privacy Policy is the exhaustive list.) The first is the GIF picker in chat, and the GIFs come from Giphy: it opens on Giphy's trending list and you can search it. Miles does not host, choose or moderate that catalogue. What we do control is the ceiling we ask for — every request is sent with rating=pg-13, which is Giphy's own classification, not ours.

The second is Watch Together, which plays a link one of you pastes. For YouTube and a few other sites that publish a player API, it is an embedded player with the two of you kept in sync. For any other link it is a web view: that page opens inside Miles and ordinary links on it work, so it behaves as a browser rather than a pinned player. Miles refuses the ways out of the app — a link that would hand off to another app, or to an app store, is cancelled — but it does not filter, rank or moderate what the site itself shows, and neither of you can be sent anything there by a third party: the only address it ever opens is the one the other of you pasted.

The third is the News cover, one of the optional cover identities in Settings. Miles installs with no cover on at all, so this applies only once someone turns that particular one on. So that it looks like what it claims to be, it fills itself with real headlines from three public news feeds — BBC News, Al Jazeera and NPR — showing each headline and, where the feed supplies one, a single thumbnail image. Miles does not write or moderate what those newsrooms publish, and shows no comments, no recommendations and nothing any user has posted; all it does with the feeds is put the newest items first and keep the first thirty. They are three established organisations operating under their own editorial standards, which is why they were chosen. The cover’s search box filters only the headlines already fetched and sends nothing to the publishers. Tapping a headline hands the address to your phone and leaves Miles entirely — it opens in your browser, or in that publisher’s own app if you have it installed, where that app’s and your device’s own protections apply. It is never displayed inside Miles.

Nothing Miles writes, and nothing any Miles user posts, is browsable, searchable or recommended anywhere.

2. Why the exposure surface is narrow, and what that does not fix

Two structural facts matter more here than any policy sentence, so they are stated first.

Adults only You must be 18 or older to hold an account. A date of birth is required to finish sign-up and the app refuses to continue if it works out to under 18; the intimacy features re-check that stored date themselves rather than trusting a flag set once. An account we learn belongs to someone under 18 is closed.
No strangers, ever An account pairs with exactly one other account, and only by an invite code one of you gives the other directly. The cap is enforced on our server, not in the app: a pairing code that would make a third member is refused outright, and each code is single-use and expires. There is no directory, no search for people, no discovery, no friend suggestions, no public profiles and no way for an unpaired account to reach you inside the app. Nobody can be contacted by a stranger in Miles, which removes the ordinary route by which grooming happens on an open network.

Neither fact is a guarantee. A date of birth entered at sign-up is a declaration, not proof of age, and we do not claim to verify it. Two people who already know each other can still pair. So the reporting route below is a real part of the system, not a formality.

3. How to report — in the app

Reporting is built into the app and needs no email, no form on this site and no account other than your own. It is reachable from three places:

  • Settings → Support → Report a problem;
  • the menu at the top of Chat, which reports the person you are paired with, and the selection toolbar you get by pressing and holding a message, which reports that message;
  • the bar at the top of the Gallery, after you press and hold an item to select it.

The report form offers a fixed list of reasons. One of them is “Sexual content involving a minor” — the CSAM category, sent to our servers as csam. Choosing it marks the report as the most serious category we have.

A report records who filed it, when, the reason chosen, what is being reported (the person you are paired with, one message, one gallery item, or Miles itself), an optional reference to that item, and an optional note of up to 1,000 characters. Who is being reported is resolved on our server from the reporter's own pairing — the app never sends it — so no account can file a report against somebody it is not paired with. If you are not paired with anyone, the report is filed against Miles rather than against a person, so the form still works before you have a partner.

Reports are write-only. There is no permission on that table for any account, including the one that filed the report: no phone can read a report back, so nothing about it can be found by someone going through a partner's device.

Two honest limits on the in-app form.

  • The report does not attach the content. It records who, when and why, not the message or the photograph. Section 4 explains why, and what it means for what we can do. If something specific matters, describe it in the note.
  • A single account is limited to five reports in 24 hours, so that the form cannot be used to flood us. The limit applies to every category including csam. If you hit it and something urgent remains unreported, email the address in section 6 — that route has no limit.

4. What we can see, and what we do about a report

This determines what enforcement is possible, so it is stated plainly rather than implied.

Nothing in Miles is proactively scanned. There is no automated content detection, no hash matching, no classifier and no moderation queue watching what two paired adults send each other. Nobody is employed to review content, and no such review happens.

Some of the app is encrypted on the sending phone — Memory Threads, the text of Wish Jar entries, the reactions put on a message, and the messages and notes written during a separation. We hold no key to those, they reach our database as ciphertext, and in the ordinary course we cannot read them.

The rest is stored so that the operator of the database could read it, though it is not read as a matter of course: chat text, chat photos, videos, voice notes and documents, the shared gallery, time capsules, and everything in the private vault — its files, its notes and its labels. The vault is guarded by a PIN, by a block on screenshots, and by access rules that admit nobody but its owner, but it is not encrypted from us, and section 5 below states what that means for a lawful request. The Privacy Policy sets out exactly which is which.

The honest limit of that encryption. So that reinstalling the app does not destroy a couple's encrypted history, a sealed copy of the device key is kept on our server, locked with a key derived from the account holder's own password. We cannot open it without that password — but the password is also what the account is signed in with, and it reaches our authentication provider each time. So the encryption is as strong as the password behind it, and we do not claim it is mathematically beyond our reach. Anyone who holds a user's password holds what opens their encrypted history.

Enforcement is therefore report-driven, and it acts on the account rather than on an individual message. It is also done by hand: there is no moderation console, no automated action and no team. Reports are read out of the database directly, with an administrative key, by the person named in section 6. When a report in the csam category arrives:

  • it is handled before every other category, and before ordinary correspondence;
  • the account reported is closed, manually, without notice and without a warning step. We do not commit to a response time, because a single named person is the whole of the process and a number we could miss would be worth less than this sentence;
  • the account details attached to the report may be passed to law enforcement, as stated in section 5 of the Terms of Use;
  • the report itself is kept, with no expiry and nothing that deletes it on a schedule. It deliberately outlives the account it was filed against — though once that account is gone the report no longer names it, and the reason, the note and the timestamp are what remain. One further limit worth knowing: a report is erased if the person who filed it deletes their own Miles account. If you have reported something serious, tell us at the address in section 6 before you delete, so the record does not go with you.

We do not scan, and we do not claim to. What we do is act decisively on what is reported, and cooperate with the authorities who can investigate what we cannot see.

5. Reporting to the authorities

Apparent child sexual abuse material reported to us is referred onward. It is not handled as an internal moderation matter and closed.

  • We report to law enforcement where the developer is established, which the Privacy Policy states is Pakistan: the National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency (NCCIA), the national cybercrime authority that absorbed the FIA Cybercrime Wing, through its complaint portal at complaint.nccia.gov.pk (guidance helpline 1799).
  • We report to the U.S. National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC) through its CyberTipline, which accepts reports from anywhere in the world.
  • Where a legal request, preservation order or lawful production order reaches us from a competent authority, we comply with it to the extent the data exists and we can read it. Chat text, chat media and Personal Vault contents we can produce. Memory Threads and Wish Jar entry text we hold only as ciphertext, and we would say so — together with the limit set out in section 4, rather than let an authority believe the material is further out of reach than it is.

If you believe a child is in immediate danger, contact your local emergency services first. Miles is not an emergency service and cannot intervene.

7. Compliance

We comply with the child safety laws applicable to Miles in the jurisdictions where it is made available, including obligations to report apparent child sexual abuse material to the competent authority, and we comply with Google Play's Child Safety Standards policy and its Child Endangerment policy.

These standards are reviewed at least once a year, and whenever the app gains a feature that changes what two people can send each other. The date at the top of this page is the date of the last review.

8. If you are the person at risk

If someone is pressuring you inside Miles, you do not have to negotiate with them to make it stop:

  • Settings → Partner → Pause notifications stops your phone being interrupted: nudges stop arriving, and an incoming call is dropped instead of ringing. Be clear about what it does not do — messages still reach your account and you will see them when you next open the app, a nudge still appears on screen if the app is already open in front of you, and a call can still post a notification even though opening it neither rings nor connects. It is silent: your partner is not told, nothing on their side shows it, and the setting is not readable by their phone.
  • Settings → Delete account (the last row on the Settings screen) ends it entirely and removes your data, as described in the Privacy Policy. If you have filed a report, read the last point of section 4 first — deleting your account deletes the report with it.
  • Reporting, as described in section 3, does not notify the person you report.
